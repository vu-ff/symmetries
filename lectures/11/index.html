Lecture 11

SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3), the double cover, and spherical harmonics

What this lecture does

Same algebra, second group. We prove that the adjoint map is a two-to-one surjection SU(2)→SO(3)\SU(2)\to\SO(3)SU(2)→SO(3) with kernel {±1}\{\pm\mathbb 1\}{±1} — the double cover — and read off its topology: SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3) is the three-sphere with antipodal points identified, and it is not simply connected. We determine which spin representations descend (exactly the integer ones), completing Lecture 9’s ledger; we realise them concretely as harmonic polynomials and spherical harmonics, proving dim⁡=2ℓ+1\dim=2\ell+1dim=2ℓ+1, irreducibility, the Laplace eigenvalue −ℓ(ℓ+1)-\ell(\ell+1)−ℓ(ℓ+1) and completeness in L2(S2)L^{2}(S^{2})L2(S2); we give Euler angles and Wigner matrices the single page they deserve; and we resolve Lecture 10’s IOU: quantum mechanics acts on rays, Wigner’s theorem makes that precise, and the physicist’s rotation group is therefore SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2).

Two groups, one algebra

Lecture 8 computed the Lie algebra of the rotation group: so(3)\so(3)so(3), the antisymmetric 3×33\times 33×3 matrices, with basis (La)jk=−ϵajk(L_a)_{jk}=-\epsilon_{ajk}(La​)jk​=−ϵajk​ and brackets [La,Lb]=ϵabcLc[L_a,L_b]=\epsilon_{abc}L_c[La​,Lb​]=ϵabc​Lc​ — the same structure constants as su(2)\su(2)su(2). Two consequences follow at once. First, the representation theory of the algebra so(3)\so(3)so(3) is already finished: transporting along ψ\psiψ and complexifying (Lecture 10’s lemma applies verbatim), the irreducible complex representations of so(3)\so(3)so(3) are the spin-jjj representations, one for every j∈12Z≥0j\in\tfrac12\Z_{\ge 0}j∈21​Z≥0​. What distinguishes them is global, and Lecture 9 taught us its name. The ledger of integration (Table 9.1) has a blank row: is SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3) simply connected, and which representations of its algebra integrate to the group? Today fills the row, and the instrument is the map ψ\psiψ promoted from algebras to groups.

The double cover

Remark 11.2 (A quotient that lands on its feet). Equation (11.1) is the only place Part II touches an abstract quotient group. Quotients of matrix groups need not be matrix groups — nothing in Lecture 2’s construction produces matrices — and our framework demands closed matrix groups. Here we are lucky twice over: the quotient is not merely isomorphic to some group, it is concretely realised, by Φ\PhiΦ itself, as the matrix group SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3). The abstract detour lasts one sentence.

Geometry first, then topology. Under the identification SU(2)≅S3\SU(2)\cong S^{3}SU(2)≅S3 of Lecture 10, the fibres of Φ\PhiΦ are the pairs {±U}\{\pm U\}{±U} — antipodal points of the sphere. So as a metric space, SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3) is the three-sphere with antipodal points glued, each rotation owning exactly two antipodal addresses upstairs. The halved angles of Lectures 8–10 are this picture in coordinates (Figure 11.1): as θ\thetaθ runs from 000 to 2π2\pi2π, the rotation Rz(θ)R_z(\theta)Rz​(θ) walks a closed loop downstairs while its address e−iθσ3/2e^{-\ii\theta\sigma_3/2}e−iθσ3​/2 walks only half of a great circle upstairs, from 1\mathbb 11 to −1-\mathbb 1−1.

Proposition 11.3 (The loops of SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3)). SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3) is connected (Lecture 8) but not simply connected: the loop ρ(t)=Rz(2πt)\rho(t)=R_z(2\pi t)ρ(t)=Rz​(2πt), t∈[0,1]t\in[0,1]t∈[0,1], is not contractible. Proof (sketch; the patching follows Lecture 9). Doubling is the easy half. The loop ρ\rhoρ traversed twice is the Φ\PhiΦ-image of u(t)=e−2πit σ3u(t)=e^{-2\pi\ii t\,\sigma_3}u(t)=e−2πitσ3​, which is a genuine loop in SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) (u(0)=u(1)=1u(0)=u(1)=\mathbb 1u(0)=u(1)=1). For the other half, lift. Around any point of SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) the map Φ\PhiΦ is locally invertible — Φ(eX)=eψ(X)\Phi(e^{X})=e^{\psi(X)}Φ(eX)=eψ(X) together with Lecture 8’s exponential charts shows it is a local homeomorphism with two continuous local inverses, differing by the sign of the sheet — so paths and homotopies in SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3) lift to SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) by exactly the subdivide-and-patch argument that lifted circle loops in Lecture 9. Contradiction. 

Remark 11.4 (The belt trick). Proposition 11.3 can be held in the hands. Give a belt a full 2π2\pi2π twist while keeping both ends fixed: no amount of manoeuvring the strap (= homotopy of the path of frames) removes the twist. Give it 4π4\pi4π, and the twist can be undone — Dirac’s belt trick (Figure 11.2). Notebook 11 animates both, next to the corresponding paths in SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2).

So SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) and SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3) share an algebra but not their loops, and by Lecture 9 that is precisely the difference that representation theory feels.

Lecture 9’s ledger closes: SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3) is not simply connected, and the algebra representations that integrate to it are exactly those built from integer spins. The algebra could not see this — ψ\psiψ is an isomorphism — the loop legislated. And the verdict agrees with analysis: Lecture 9 derived integer quantisation of orbital angular momentum from single-valuedness on a circle of rotations; today’s theorem is the same fact read globally, since function actions are honest SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3) representations and must therefore carry integer spin only. Where half-integers live we already know (Lecture 10); why they are lawful is Section 11.6. First, the integer representations deserve their concrete home.

Rays, projective representations, and Wigner’s theorem

One debt remains, and it is conceptual. Lecture 10 took the symmetry group of a quantum system to be SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) while geometry insisted on SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3), and promised this lecture would arbitrate. The arbitration turns on what a quantum state actually is.

A state is not a vector. Two unit vectors differing by a phase, ψ\psiψ and eiαψe^{\ii\alpha}\psieiαψ, are indistinguishable by any measurement: every prediction of quantum mechanics is built from transition probabilities P([φ],[ψ])=∣⟨φ|ψ⟩∣2,P\bigl([\varphi],[\psi]\bigr)=\abs{\braket{\varphi}{\psi}}^{2},P([φ],[ψ])=∣⟨φ∣ψ⟩∣2, which depend only on the rays [ψ]={eiαψ:α∈R}[\psi]=\{e^{\ii\alpha}\psi:\alpha\in\R\}[ψ]={eiαψ:α∈R}. The honest state space is the set of unit rays, and the honest notion of symmetry follows: a quantum symmetry is a bijection TTT of the rays preserving all transition probabilities. What such maps can be is settled by a theorem we import — the last conceptual import of the course.

Theorem 11.18 (Wigner’s theorem; imported). Every quantum symmetry TTT is implemented by an operator U ⁣:H→HU\colon\mathcal H\to\mathcal HU:H→H with T[ψ]=[Uψ]T[\psi]=[U\psi]T[ψ]=[Uψ], where UUU is either unitary or antiunitary — additive, conjugate-linear, with ⟨Uφ|Uψ⟩=⟨φ|ψ⟩‾\braket{U\varphi}{U\psi}=\overline{\braket{\varphi}{\psi}}⟨Uφ∣Uψ⟩=⟨φ∣ψ⟩​ — and UUU is unique up to a phase.

Reference for the omitted proof: Weinberg, The Quantum Theory of Fields I, Appendix 2.A.

Antiunitary implementers are not exotica — time reversal is one, as Sheet 7 noted — but they cannot occur in a connected symmetry group: every element of a connected matrix Lie group is a finite product of exponentials (Lecture 8), hence of squares eX=(eX/2)2e^{X}=(e^{X/2})^{2}eX=(eX/2)2, and the implementer of a square, Uh2=ω UhUhU_{h^{2}}=\omega\,U_hU_hUh2​=ωUh​Uh​, is unitary whether UhU_hUh​ is unitary or antiunitary. For rotations, then, only unitaries — but unitaries defined up to phases, and the phases are the whole story.

Definition 11.19 (Projective representation). A projective unitary representation of a group GGG on H\mathcal HH is a map g↦Ugg\mapsto U_gg↦Ug​ into the unitaries with Ug Uh=ω(g,h) Ugh,∣ω(g,h)∣=1: U_g\,U_h=\omega(g,h)\,U_{gh}, \qquad \abs{\omega(g,h)}=1 : Ug​Uh​=ω(g,h)Ugh​,∣ω(g,h)∣=1: a representation up to phases — exactly what Wigner’s theorem delivers when a group acts by quantum symmetries.

Here, at last, is the arbitration. Quantum mechanics is formulated on rays; its symmetry groups act by projective representations; and the projective representations of SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3) are precisely the honest representations of SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) — the half we proved above, the converse (every projective representation of SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3) arises so, after adjusting phases) being Bargmann’s theorem, stated as Theorem 11.21 below and imported. The physicist’s rotation group is SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2), not by decree but because the quantum kinematics of rays makes the simply connected cover available free of charge. Lecture 10’s IOU is paid in full: a spin-12\tfrac1221​ system violates nothing; it merely uses the phases that vectors carry and rays forgive. Applied to SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3), whose universal cover is SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) with su(2)\su(2)su(2) semisimple, this supplies exactly the converse used above: every projective representation of SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3) arises, after adjusting phases, from an honest representation of SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2). (The Galilei group fails the hypothesis — H2≠0H^{2}\neq0H2=0, the surviving cocycle being the mass — which is why its phases do not lift. And the minus sign is not philosophy. In 1975 two independent crystal-neutron-interferometer experiments made it relative and therefore observable. Rauch and collaborators coherently split unpolarised slow neutrons, varied the unequal magnetic-field integrals along the two paths, and observed output-intensity oscillations with the predicted 4π4\pi4π spinor period. Werner and collaborators instead used a polarised beam and measured the interferometric phase shift produced by magnetic precession, directly identifying the sign reversal after a 2π2\pi2π rotation. Both experiments use magnetic precession and path recombination, but they are not the same protocol. The (−1)2j(-1)^{2j}(−1)2j of Lecture 10, computed from bare algebra, is visible in a reactor hall.

  • Spherical harmonics. The degree-ℓ\ellℓ harmonic polynomials carry the spin-ℓ\ellℓ representation, of dimension 2ℓ+12\ell+12ℓ+1 (Theorem 11.9, Proposition 11.8), with spherical-Laplacian eigenvalue −ℓ(ℓ+1)-\ell(\ell+1)−ℓ(ℓ+1) (Theorem 11.12).
  • Completeness. The YℓmY_{\ell m}Yℓm​ form an orthonormal basis of L2(S2)L^2(S^2)L2(S2) (Theorem 11.14); Euler angles and the Wigner matrices coordinatise rotations (Proposition 11.15).
  • Projective representations. Quantum states are rays (Wigner’s theorem, Theorem 11.18), so half-integer spin is lawful and SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) is the operative group (Proposition 11.20).
