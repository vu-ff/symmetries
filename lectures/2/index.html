Lecture 2

Structure of groups, and a first look at crystallographic groups

What this lecture does

We learn to compare groups by structure-preserving maps and to take groups apart: homomorphisms and their kernels, conjugacy and its geometric meaning, quotient groups and the first isomorphism theorem, direct products, and the construction of semidirect products from a normal subgroup and an action, and the decomposition of a finite group into simple constituents. As a first physical payoff we prove the crystallographic restriction theorem: a crystal lattice admits only two-, three-, four- and sixfold rotation axes.

Homomorphisms

Lecture 1 declared two groups “the same” when a bijection matches their multiplication tables. Far more common, and at least as useful, are maps that preserve multiplication without being bijective: they compress a group onto a smaller one, retaining some structure and discarding the rest.

Conjugacy

Definition 2.4 (Conjugacy). Elements g,g′g,g'g,g′ of a group GGG are conjugate if g′=hgh−1g'=hgh^{-1}g′=hgh−1 for some h∈Gh\in Gh∈G. Conjugation has a sharp physical interpretation: hgh−1hgh^{-1}hgh−1 is the operation ggg as seen in the frame transformed by hhh. Indeed, when GGG consists of transformations of space, the identity (hgh−1)(hx)=h(g(x))\bigl(hgh^{-1}\bigr)(hx)=h\bigl(g(x)\bigr)(hgh−1)(hx)=h(g(x)) says that hgh−1hgh^{-1}hgh−1 does to the transformed point hxhxhx exactly what ggg does to xxx: transform back, apply ggg, transform forward. Conjugate operations are therefore the same kind of operation, differing only in where they sit — a rotation through θ\thetaθ about one axis is conjugate to the rotation through θ\thetaθ about any other axis the group can reach. Quantities that physics should not care about the placement of — characters, in Lecture 5 — will be constant on conjugacy classes.

In the orthonormal basis RRR displays its angle; in the lattice basis it displays its integrality; the trace, blind to basis, forces the two displays to be compatible. This “evaluate an invariant in two ways” manoeuvre will return many times, most spectacularly with characters in Lecture 5.

Remark 2.35 (Fivefold symmetry and quasicrystals). The missing fivefold axis is the group-theoretic face of a familiar fact: regular pentagons do not tile the plane. Nature, however, found a loophole. In 1982 Shechtman observed metallic alloys whose diffraction patterns show sharp peaks with tenfold symmetry — crisp long-range order without lattice periodicity. These quasicrystals (Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 2011), like the Penrose tilings that model them, evade the restriction theorem by giving up the lattice Λ\LambdaΛ, not by violating the mathematics: the theorem is about periodic structures and says nothing about aperiodic ones.

The classification logic

The restriction theorem is the engine of a complete classification, whose shape we now sketch; the details belong to crystallography, but the logic is pure group theory.

First, one needs the finite rotation groups available in three dimensions — the analogue of Leonardo’s theorem from Lecture 1:

Theorem 2.36 (Finite subgroups of SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3); statement only). Every finite subgroup of SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3) is one of: the cyclic groups CnC_nCn​; the dihedral groups DnD_nDn​ (realised by nnn-gons lying in space); the rotation group T≅A4T\cong A_4T≅A4​ of the regular tetrahedron (order 121212); the rotation group O≅S4O\cong S_4O≅S4​ of the cube and octahedron (order 242424); and the rotation group Y≅A5Y\cong A_5Y≅A5​ of the icosahedron and dodecahedron (order 606060).

Molecules realise the exotic entries: methane’s rotations form TTT, sulphur hexafluoride’s form OOO, and the fullerene C60_{60}60​ realises YYY. For crystals, however, Theorem 2.34 expels every group containing a fivefold rotation or any rotation of order ≥7\geq 7≥7: the icosahedral group is forbidden, and only n∈{1,2,3,4,6}n\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}n∈{1,2,3,4,6} survive in the cyclic and dihedral families. Adjoining improper operations costs nothing new: every lattice satisfies −Λ=Λ-\Lambda=\Lambda−Λ=Λ, so the inversion −1-\mathbb 1−1 lies in P(Λ)P(\Lambda)P(Λ) for every lattice, and any improper AAA factors through it as A=(−1)(−A)A=(-\mathbb 1)(-A)A=(−1)(−A) with −A-A−A a rotation, exactly as in Example 2.23 — so the improper possibilities are governed by the proper ones. Assembling the allowed proper and improper combinations yields:

Theorem 2.37 (Hessel; statement only). Up to equivalence there are exactly 323232 finite subgroups of O(3)\Orth(3)O(3) compatible with a three-dimensional lattice: the 323232 crystallographic point groups, or crystal classes.

The corresponding count of essentially different lattices is the 141414 Bravais lattices; and combining point operations with translations (including the hybrid screw rotations and glide reflections) yields the 230230230 space groups of Fedorov and Schoenflies — the complete catalogue of symmetries available to any crystal in nature. In two dimensions the same programme gives 101010 point groups, 555 Bravais lattices and the 171717 wallpaper groups. We will not need these lists; what we will need, from Lecture 6 onwards, is the representation theory of the individual point groups, where molecules like H2_22​O and NH3_33​ from Lecture 1 reappear with their vibrational spectra. The bridge from groups to spectra — linear representations — is the subject of Lecture 3.

Summary

  • Homomorphism. A product-preserving map; its kernel is normal and its image a subgroup (Definition 2.1, Proposition 2.3).
  • Conjugacy. Conjugacy classes partition the group; in SnS_nSn​ they are the cycle types (Proposition 2.9), and class sizes divide ∣G∣\lvert G\rvert∣G∣ (Proposition 2.12).
  • Quotients. A normal subgroup yields a quotient group (Theorem 2.16); the first isomorphism theorem gives G/ker⁡φ≅im⁡φG/\ker\varphi\cong\im\varphiG/kerφ≅imφ (Theorem 2.18).
