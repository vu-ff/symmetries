Lecture 15

The Poincaré group and the classification of particles

What this lecture does

The finale executes the program. Adding translations to the Lorentz group gives the Poincaré group — a semidirect product in the sense of Lecture 2, realised as 5×55\times55×5 matrices — and the fields-versus-states principle of Lecture 14 says that its infinite-dimensional unitary irreducible representations are the elementary particles. We classify them by Wigner’s method: diagonalise the translations, watch momenta sweep out orbits — mass shells and the light cone, with their invariant measures derived separately — and reduce everything to the little group of one reference momentum: SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) for massive particles, whence spin; the double cover of the Euclidean plane group for massless ones, whence helicity and the photon’s two polarisations, alongside the mathematically allowed continuous-spin branch. For massive particles the Casimirs recover mass and spin; for finite-helicity massless particles both Casimirs vanish and helicity remains a little-group character. A one-page retrospect closes the course by stating its boundary: the representation-theoretic vocabulary needed for SU(3)\SU(3)SU(3) has been prepared, while particle-physics SU(3)\SU(3)SU(3) itself is assigned to explicit further reading.

Wigner’s definition closes the circle: an elementary particle is a system whose state space carries an irreducible such representation — elementary because irreducible: no invariant subspace, no way to take it apart. Lecture 14 proved these representations must be infinite-dimensional; Lecture 13 taught us not to flinch. The rest of the lecture classifies them.

Massless particles: helicity

The massless story differs in exactly one ingredient: the little group is E~(2)\widetilde{\mathrm E}(2)E(2), and its representation theory has a fork in it.

Nature’s choice at the fork is empirical and unanimous: every massless particle observed is of type (i). The continuous-spin option — infinitely many polarisation states at each momentum — is a chair at Wigner’s table that has never been filled.

Helicity’s physics is its behaviour under parity. Spatial inversion reverses p⃗\vec pp​ but not the axial J⃗\vec JJ, so it flips J⃗⋅p^\vec J\cdot\hat pJ⋅p^​: helicity hhh becomes −h-h−h (at group level, conjugation by parity carries the little group of p^\hat pp^​ to that of the reversed ray, inverting the rotation character). A parity-symmetric theory must therefore contain ±h\pm h±h together, and electromagnetism is the example: the photon is the pair h=±1h=\pm1h=±1, and so has exactly two polarisation states — where a massive spin-one particle has 2j+1=32j+1=32j+1=3. The missing longitudinal photon is a little-group theorem: the third state is a luxury only mass can buy. (The graviton’s pair is h=±2h=\pm2h=±2; the weak interaction, which violates parity, happily employs unpaired helicities.) One last ledger entry: the photon state has one component per momentum and helicity, while the photon field AμA_\muAμ​ has four — the mismatch between (12,12)(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)(21​,21​) and χ±1\chi_{\pm1}χ±1​ is real, and the apparatus that manages it is gauge invariance: the final, and deepest, item in the fields-versus-states accounts, payable in a field theory course.

Retrospect

The positive-energy catalogue is now separated into its branches. An elementary relativistic system is an orbit and a little-group representation: massive particles (m,j)(m,j)(m,j), with spin defined by the rest frame’s SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2); massless particles (0,h)(0,h)(0,h), with helicity defined by the ray’s E~(2)\widetilde{\mathrm E}(2)E(2) rotation character; the two continuous-spin families at each ρ>0\rho>0ρ>0, distinguished by the cover’s central sign; and the vacuum, alone on the trivial orbit. In the empirical table below two chairs stand empty: the continuous-spin representations, and the spacelike orbits — whose little group, the stabiliser of X(p^)=μσ3X(\hat p)=\mu\sigma_3X(p^​)=μσ3​ in SL(2,C)\SL(2,\C)SL(2,C), is the noncompact group of σ3\sigma_3σ3​-unitaries SU(1,1)≅SL(2,R)\SU(1,1)\cong\SL(2,\R)SU(1,1)≅SL(2,R), the double cover of the geometric little group SO+(1,2)\SO^{+}(1,2)SO+(1,2), so that nontrivial unitary little-group representations would give tachyons infinitely many spin states on top of their causal misbehaviour. The trivial unitary representation is the one-dimensional exception. Nature, as far as anyone has looked, declines both.

The one historical correction sits in the second row: neutrinos were long entered as massless (0,±12)(0,\pm\tfrac12)(0,±21​), but flavour oscillations prove mν≠0m_\nu\neq0mν​=0, moving them onto the massive ladder — the single empirical edit made to Wigner’s 1939 table.

What remains between this classification and physics is one construction: a quantum field is a spacetime-indexed family of Fock-space operators intertwining a finite-dimensional (j1,j2)(j_1,j_2)(j1​,j2​) — Lecture 14’s fields — with the infinite-dimensional unitary representations built today — Lecture 15’s states. The Dirac equation was the prototype of such an intertwining condition; building them systematically, with locality and causality enforced, is quantum field theory, the natural next course. Its multi-particle arena, Fock space with Γ\GammaΓ and dΓ\dGammadΓ, left Lecture 13 ready for use.

The course’s SU(3)\SU(3)SU(3) boundary is deliberate. Duals and conjugate representations, invariant tensors, SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) coupling, and highest-weight staircases have prepared the vocabulary and methods. Applying them to the quark 3\mathbf 33 and antiquark 3ˉ\bar{\mathbf 3}3ˉ, the eightfold way, roots and weights, and strong-interaction invariant tensors is further study, not material taught in these lectures. Jones, Ch. 8, §§8.3–8.4, pp. 149–164 is the exact next destination. The other horizons are visible from here: symmetry made local (gauge theory), symmetry broken by a ground state, harmonic analysis and Mackey theory in earnest, and the strange cases where a classical symmetry fails to survive quantisation.

We opened Part I by counting the symmetries of a triangle; we close Part II by defining what a particle is. It was the same calculation throughout — decompose, then intertwine — performed with heavier measures and steadier nerves, and its moral has not changed since the third week: a symmetry is never just a group; it is a group represented, and the representation is where the physics lives.

Summary

  • Poincaré group. The semidirect product R1,3⋊SO+(1,3)\R^{1,3}\rtimes\SO^{+}(1,3)R1,3⋊SO+(1,3) of translations and Lorentz transformations (eq. (15.1)).
  • Orbits. The Lorentz orbits in momentum space are the mass shells, the light cone, the spacelike sheet, and the origin (Proposition 15.1); the massive-shell and punctured-cone measures are derived separately in Propositions 15.2 and 15.3.
  • Little groups. The stabiliser of a momentum is SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) for a massive particle (Proposition 15.5) and E~(2)\widetilde{\mathrm E}(2)E(2) for a massless one (Proposition 15.6).
  • Wigner classification. An elementary particle is an orbit together with an irreducible representation of its little group (Mackey, Theorem 15.7): massive (m,j)(m,j)(m,j) with spin (Theorem 15.8); massless finite-helicity characters (0,h)(0,h)(0,h); and two continuous-spin sectors for each ρ>0\rho>0ρ>0.
  • Casimirs. On the massive branch, P^ 2=m2\widehat P^{\,2}=m^2P2=m2 and W^ 2=−m2j(j+1)\widehat W^{\,2}=-m^2j(j+1)W2=−m2j(j+1) recover mass and spin. On the finite-helicity massless branch W^μ=hP^μ\widehat W^\mu=h\widehat P^\muWμ=hPμ, so both Casimirs vanish and the rotation character, not the Casimirs, records hhh.
