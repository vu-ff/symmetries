Lecture 13

The Heisenberg group, the oscillator, and Fock space

What this lecture does

Until now every representation in this course has been finite- dimensional by choice. From here on it is infinite-dimensional by theorem: a two-line trace argument shows that the canonical commutation relation [Q,P]=iℏ 1[Q,P]=\ii\hbar\,\mathbb 1[Q,P]=iℏ1 has no finite-dimensional realisation at all. We package the relation as a Lie algebra and a matrix group — the Heisenberg group, on whose nilpotent algebra the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff series terminates and becomes a theorem of this course; we construct the Schrödinger representation on L2(R)L^{2}(\R)L2(R), state Stone–von Neumann uniqueness, and read off why quantum mechanics is one theory; we solve the harmonic oscillator by pure ladder algebra, with coherent states appearing as an orbit of the group; and we let Part I’s symmetric group return to govern many particles — bosonic and fermionic Fock spaces, canonical commutation and anticommutation relations, and second quantisation, with a one-page glance at fields to set up the finale.

Remark 13.3 (The centre is made of phases). By (13.3), two Heisenberg group elements built from XXX- and YYY-directions commute only up to a factor from the centre. In a unitary representation, Schur’s lemma will force the central ecZe^{cZ}ecZ to act as mere phases — precisely the currency that Lecture 11 showed quantum mechanics keeps in circulation: states are rays, and phases are free. The Heisenberg centre is thus the abstract home of quantum phases; the irremovable mass-phase of the Galilei group that Lecture 11 mentioned in passing is this centre’s close kin, not the same central character: Galilei mass is the eigenvalue of the Galilei central generator, whereas the Heisenberg central character below is labelled by ℏ\hbarℏ. Translations of position and translations of momentum commute as physical operations; their quantum implementers do not, and the mismatch is a phase — measurable, as interferometry keeps demonstrating.

No finite-dimensional escape, and the Schrödinger representation

Infinite-dimensionality of quantum mechanics is not mathematical extravagance; it is a theorem, and the rest of the lecture lives with its consequences.

Theorem 13.7 (Stone–von Neumann; imported). Every strongly continuous irreducible unitary representation of H3H_3H3​ on a separable Hilbert space whose central subgroup acts by the phases e−ic/ℏe^{-\ii c/\hbar}e−ic/ℏ, for a fixed ℏ≠0\hbar\neq 0ℏ=0, is unitarily equivalent to the Schrödinger representation.

Reference for the omitted proof: Folland, Harmonic Analysis in Phase Space, Theorem 1.50; Woit, the Heisenberg-group chapters. The full Folland entry appears in the consolidated bibliography.

This is the uniqueness theorem behind the sentence “quantum mechanics is one theory”. In 1925–26 Heisenberg’s matrix mechanics and Schrödinger’s wave mechanics computed the same spectra by what looked like different physics; Theorem 13.7 says that any two realisations of the same canonical commutation relations are the same theory in different unitary coordinates. (Starred, and ominous: the theorem fails for infinitely many degrees of freedom. Quantum field theory lives with inequivalent representations of its commutation relations — inequivalent vacua — and Section 13.5 will peek through that door.)

Many particles: the symmetric group returns

One particle has a Hilbert space H\mathcal HH; nnn particles of the same kind have H⊗n\mathcal H^{\otimes n}H⊗n, and on it two groups act at once. The symmetric group SnS_nSn​ — Part I’s protagonist — permutes the factors, Pσ (v1⊗⋯⊗vn)=vσ−1(1)⊗⋯⊗vσ−1(n),P_\sigma\,(v_1\otimes\cdots\otimes v_n) =v_{\sigma^{-1}(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{\sigma^{-1}(n)},Pσ​(v1​⊗⋯⊗vn​)=vσ−1(1)​⊗⋯⊗vσ−1(n)​, and any one-particle symmetry acts diagonally, U↦U⊗nU\mapsto U^{\otimes n}U↦U⊗n. The two actions commute — permuting identical copies of UUU does nothing — and that innocuous sentence is the skeleton of many-body physics. Inside H⊗n\mathcal H^{\otimes n}H⊗n sit the symmetric and antisymmetric subspaces, Sym⁡nH={ψ:Pσψ=ψ ∀σ},ΛnH={ψ:Pσψ=sgn⁡(σ) ψ ∀σ},\operatorname{Sym}^{n}\mathcal H =\{\psi:P_\sigma\psi=\psi\ \forall\sigma\}, \qquad \Lambda^{n}\mathcal H =\{\psi:P_\sigma\psi=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\,\psi\ \forall\sigma\},SymnH={ψ:Pσ​ψ=ψ ∀σ},ΛnH={ψ:Pσ​ψ=sgn(σ)ψ ∀σ}, the isotypic components of the two one-dimensional representations of SnS_nSn​, both invariant under every U⊗nU^{\otimes n}U⊗n because the actions commute.

Now the physics. For identical particles, permutations relabel our bookkeeping, so physical observables are permutation-invariant and the state space splits into SnS_nSn​ representation sectors. Ray invariance alone does not force every ray to carry a one-dimensional character: higher-dimensional irreducible sectors are also allowed and are called parastatistics. Permutation type is a superselection label under permutation-invariant observables; that statement must not be confused with selecting only two types.

For ordinary nonrelativistic quantum mechanics we therefore add the symmetrisation postulate as physical input: a species is assigned either the trivial SnS_nSn​ sector Sym⁡nH\operatorname{Sym}^{n}\mathcal HSymnH (bosons) or the sign sector ΛnH\Lambda^{n}\mathcal HΛnH (fermions). This is the adopted input for the Fock-space construction below, not a consequence of ray kinematics. The relativistic spin–statistics theorem, which relates spin to that assignment under additional physical hypotheses, is a distinct result outside this course’s proof scope. (In two space dimensions exchange is braided rather than permutational, allowing anyons; starred reading.) Messiah–Greenberg is the source for the independent nonrelativistic symmetrisation postulate and its experimental foundation; it is not being cited as a proof of relativistic spin–statistics. For n=2n=2n=2, H=C2\mathcal H=\C^{2}H=C2, this is Lecture 12’s singlet–triplet split: the duet, now a chorus. Starred, for perspective: the full decomposition of (Cd)⊗n(\C^{d})^{\otimes n}(Cd)⊗n under the commuting pair Sn×U(d)S_n\times\U(d)Sn​×U(d) pairs their irreducible representations one-to-one through a single combinatorics — Schur–Weyl duality, the proper sequel to Part I.

Outlook: fields

One page of honesty about where this leads. Put a free scalar particle on a ring of length LLL: the one-particle space has modes eikxe^{\ii k x}eikx with k∈2πLZk\in\frac{2\pi}{L}\Zk∈L2π​Z, and a free field is, by definition, the bosonic Fock construction applied to all of them at once, with Hamiltonian H=∑kℏωk(ak†ak+12):H=\sum_{k}\hbar\omega_k \bigl(a_k^{\dagger}a_k+\tfrac12\bigr) :H=k∑​ℏωk​(ak†​ak​+21​): a field is countably many oscillators. For any finite set of modes, everything in this lecture is rigorous and nothing new happens. The new phenomena are exactly the infinities. First, the zero-point sum ∑kℏωk/2\sum_k\hbar\omega_k/2∑k​ℏωk​/2 diverges — the first renormalisation: energies are measured from the vacuum, so one subtracts it (“normal ordering”), though its variation with boundary conditions survives as measurable physics, the Casimir effect. Second, and deeper, Stone–von Neumann fails: for infinitely many modes the CCR algebra has inequivalent irreducible representations, and physically distinct situations — different masses, different phases of matter, interacting versus free vacua — inhabit different ones. The uniqueness that made quantum mechanics one theory makes quantum field theory many; taming that plurality is a different course. What this course can still settle is the question the construction begs: a relativistic field should carry a representation of which group? Of the symmetries of spacetime — and so the finale begins: the Lorentz group (Lecture 14) and the Poincaré group (Lecture 15), where the infinite-dimensional unitary representations this lecture has normalised stop being optional.

Summary

  • No-go. The canonical relation [Q,P]=iℏ1[Q,P]=\ii\hbar\mathbb 1[Q,P]=iℏ1 has no finite-dimensional representation (the trace obstruction, Theorem 13.4).
  • Heisenberg group. Step-two nilpotent, so Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff terminates (Proposition 13.1, Theorem 13.2), giving the Weyl form of the relations (eq. (13.4)).
  • Uniqueness. The Schrödinger representation on L2(R)L^2(\R)L2(R) (Proposition 13.6) is the unique irreducible one (Stone–von Neumann, Theorem 13.7).
  • Oscillator and coherent states. Ladder operators fix the spectrum by algebra alone (Theorem 13.8); coherent states are the orbit of the vacuum under the Heisenberg group (Proposition 13.9).
  • Fock space. Bosonic and fermionic Fock spaces realise the CCR and CAR (Definition 13.11, Proposition 13.12); symmetries lift by second quantisation dΓ\dd\GammadΓ (Theorem 13.14).
