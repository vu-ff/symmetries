Groups, examples, and the landscape of finite groups

What this lecture does

We abstract the group axioms from the behaviour of symmetry operations, assemble a working zoo of finite groups — cyclic, dihedral and symmetric — together with a first glimpse of the matrix groups of Part II, make precise when two groups are “the same”, and prove the fundamental counting theorem of the subject, Lagrange’s theorem.

From symmetry operations to the group axioms

Symmetry, in physics as in geometry, is always symmetry under something: a transformation that leaves the relevant structure unchanged. The structure may be a geometric figure, a crystal lattice, a molecule’s equilibrium configuration, or — as we shall see at the end of Part I — the Hamiltonian of a physical system. The first task of this course is to extract, from the behaviour of such transformations, a small set of axioms, and then to develop the mathematics of those axioms far enough that it starts returning concrete physical predictions: degeneracies of energy levels, patterns of vibrational spectra, selection rules.

We begin with the simplest nontrivial example. Let Δ\DeltaΔ be an equilateral triangle in the plane, its vertices labelled 1,2,31,2,31,2,3. A symmetry operation of Δ\DeltaΔ is a distance-preserving map of the plane that carries Δ\DeltaΔ onto itself. There are exactly six (Figure 1.1):

  • the identity map eee, which moves nothing;

  • the rotations rrr and r2r^2r2 about the centroid, through 2π/32\pi/32π/3 and 4π/34\pi/34π/3 anticlockwise;

  • the reflections σ1,σ2,σ3\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3σ1​,σ2​,σ3​ in the three axes joining a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side.

Each operation is determined by what it does to the vertices. Writing g ⁣:i↦jg\colon i\mapsto jg:i↦j when ggg carries vertex iii to the position of vertex jjj, we have r ⁣:1↦2,  2↦3,  3↦1,σ1 ⁣:1↦1,  2↦3,  3↦2,r\colon 1\mapsto 2,\; 2\mapsto 3,\; 3\mapsto 1, \qquad \sigma_1\colon 1\mapsto 1,\; 2\mapsto 3,\; 3\mapsto 2,r:1↦2,2↦3,3↦1,σ1​:1↦1,2↦3,3↦2, and similarly for the rest. Two operations performed in succession are again a symmetry operation. Throughout the course, composition is written right to left: ghghgh means “first hhh, then ggg”, so that (gh)(x)=g(h(x))(gh)(x)=g\bigl(h(x)\bigr)(gh)(x)=g(h(x)) for points xxx. Tracing vertices, rσ1 ⁣:1↦σ11↦r2,2↦3↦1,3↦2↦3,r\sigma_1\colon 1\xmapsto{\sigma_1}1\xmapsto{r}2,\quad 2\mapsto 3\mapsto 1,\quad 3\mapsto 2\mapsto 3,rσ1​:1σ1​​1r​2,2↦3↦1,3↦2↦3, so rσ1r\sigma_1rσ1​ fixes vertex 333 and swaps 1,21,21,2: it equals σ3\sigma_3σ3​. The same computation in the other order gives σ1r=σ2\sigma_1 r=\sigma_2σ1​r=σ2​. Hence rσ1=σ3≠σ2=σ1r:r\sigma_1=\sigma_3\neq\sigma_2=\sigma_1 r:rσ1​=σ3​=σ2​=σ1​r: the order in which symmetry operations are performed matters. This noncommutativity is not a defect; it is the source of most of the interesting structure in this course.

Four features of this example are completely general. The composition of two symmetry operations is a symmetry operation (closure); composition of maps is associative; the identity map is a symmetry operation; and every symmetry operation can be undone by a symmetry operation (inverses). These four properties are all that the abstract theory retains, and they are the content of the next definition.

The definition of a group

Definition 1.1 (Group). A group is a set GGG together with a map G×G→GG\times G\to GG×G→G, (g,h)↦gh(g,h)\mapsto gh(g,h)↦gh, called the group multiplication, such that:

  1. Associativity: (gh)k=g(hk)(gh)k=g(hk)(gh)k=g(hk) for all g,h,k∈Gg,h,k\in Gg,h,k∈G.

  2. Identity: there exists e∈Ge\in Ge∈G with eg=ge=geg=ge=geg=ge=g for all g∈Gg\in Gg∈G.

  3. Inverses: for every g∈Gg\in Gg∈G there exists g−1∈Gg^{-1}\in Gg−1∈G with gg−1=g−1g=egg^{-1}=g^{-1}g=egg−1=g−1g=e.

Remark 1.2 (Conventions). Closure is built into the definition: the multiplication is required to take values in GGG. We write the multiplication by juxtaposition and call ghghgh the product, even when the operation is composition of maps or addition of numbers. The identity is denoted eee throughout these notes; the physics and chemistry literature often writes EEE, and algebra texts sometimes write 111. By associativity, products such as ghkghkghk need no brackets, and powers gn=g⋯gg^n=g\cdots ggn=g⋯g (nnn factors), g0=eg^0=eg0=e, g−n=(g−1)ng^{-n}=(g^{-1})^ng−n=(g−1)n obey the usual rules of exponents.

Definition 1.3 (Abelian group). A group GGG is abelian (or commutative) if gh=hggh=hggh=hg for all g,h∈Gg,h\in Gg,h∈G. For abelian groups one sometimes uses additive notation: g+hg+hg+h for the product, 000 for the identity, −g-g−g for the inverse.

Example 1.4. (i) The integers Z\ZZ under addition form an abelian group: the identity is 000 and the inverse of nnn is −n-n−n. Likewise R\RR and C\CC under addition. (ii) The nonzero rationals Q∖{0}\Q\setminus\{0\}Q∖{0} under multiplication form an abelian group, as do R∖{0}\R\setminus\{0\}R∖{0} and C∖{0}\C\setminus\{0\}C∖{0}; the two-element set {1,−1}\{1,-1\}{1,−1} is a group under multiplication. (iii) The natural numbers N\NN under addition are not a group (no inverses), and Z\ZZ under multiplication is not a group (222 has no integer inverse). (iv) The six symmetry operations of the triangle form a nonabelian group under composition, as we verified above.

A zoo of examples

Group theory is learnt from examples. The three families introduced in this section — cyclic, dihedral and symmetric groups — will be our laboratory for the whole of Part I.

Cyclic groups

Definition 1.8 (Cyclic group). A group GGG is cyclic if there is an element g∈Gg\in Gg∈G, called a generator, such that every element of GGG is a power of ggg: G={ gk:k∈Z }.G=\{\,g^k : k\in\Z\,\}.G={gk:k∈Z}. The cyclic group with nnn elements is denoted CnC_nCn​.

If a generator ggg has order nnn, then by Proposition 1.7 the powers e,g,g2,…,gn−1e,g,g^2,\dots,g^{n-1}e,g,g2,…,gn−1 are distinct and exhaust the group, and gagb=ga+bg^a g^b=g^{a+b}gagb=ga+b with the exponent read modulo nnn. The abstract group CnC_nCn​ appears in many concrete guises:

  1. the rotations of the plane through multiples of 2π/n2\pi/n2π/n about a fixed point, under composition;

  2. the nnn-th roots of unity {1,ω,ω2,…,ωn−1}⊂C\{1,\omega,\omega^2,\dots,\omega^{n-1}\}\subset\C{1,ω,ω2,…,ωn−1}⊂C, where ω=e2πi/n\omega=e^{2\pi\ii/n}ω=e2πi/n, under multiplication;

  3. the residues {0,1,…,n−1}\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}{0,1,…,n−1} under addition modulo nnn, written Zn\Z_nZn​.

That these are “the same group” is made precise in Section 1.3.4. Every cyclic group is abelian, since gagb=ga+b=gbgag^ag^b=g^{a+b}=g^bg^agagb=ga+b=gbga. The integers Z\ZZ under addition are the infinite cyclic group: every element is a multiple of the generator 111.

Symmetric groups

Definition 1.13 (Symmetric group). The symmetric group SnS_nSn​ is the set of all bijections (permutations) σ ⁣:{1,…,n}→{1,…,n}\sigma\colon\{1,\dots,n\}\to\{1,\dots,n\}σ:{1,…,n}→{1,…,n}, under composition of maps.

Proof. Composites and inverses of bijections are bijections, composition of maps is associative, and the identity map is a bijection; so SnS_nSn​ is a group. Proof. Hence P(a b)Δ=−ΔP_{(a\,b)}\Delta=-\DeltaP(ab)​Δ=−Δ. The final statement follows from multiplicativity. 

Permutations of sign +1+1+1 are called even, those of sign −1-1−1 odd. A kkk-cycle is a product of k−1k-1k−1 transpositions (Proposition 1.17), hence has sign (−1)k−1(-1)^{k-1}(−1)k−1. The sign of a permutation will reappear throughout the course — most immediately in Lecture 3, where sgn⁡\sgnsgn is our first example of a nontrivial one-dimensional representation, and in Part II in the guise of the Levi-Civita symbol.

Comparing groups: isomorphism

The rotations through multiples of 2π/32\pi/32π/3, the cube roots of unity, and the residues {0,1,2}\{0,1,2\}{0,1,2} modulo 333 are visibly “the same group” in three different costumes: their elements match up so that the multiplication tables coincide. The following definition makes this precise.

Definition 1.20 (Isomorphism). An isomorphism between groups GGG and HHH is a bijection φ ⁣:G→H\varphi\colon G\to Hφ:G→H that preserves multiplication: φ(g1g2)=φ(g1) φ(g2)for all g1,g2∈G.\varphi(g_1g_2)=\varphi(g_1)\,\varphi(g_2) \qquad\text{for all } g_1,g_2\in G .φ(g1​g2​)=φ(g1​)φ(g2​)for all g1​,g2​∈G. If an isomorphism exists, GGG and HHH are isomorphic, written G≅HG\cong HG≅H.

An isomorphism automatically matches identities with identities and inverses with inverses: from φ(e)φ(e)=φ(e)\varphi(e)\varphi(e)=\varphi(e)φ(e)φ(e)=φ(e) and cancellation in HHH, φ(e)=eH\varphi(e)=e_Hφ(e)=eH​; then φ(g)φ(g−1)=φ(e)=eH\varphi(g)\varphi(g^{-1})=\varphi(e)=e_Hφ(g)φ(g−1)=φ(e)=eH​ gives φ(g−1)=φ(g)−1\varphi(g^{-1})=\varphi(g)^{-1}φ(g−1)=φ(g)−1.

Example 1.21. The three realisations of CnC_nCn​ in Section 1.3.1 are isomorphic: writing ρk\rho_kρk​ for rotation through 2πk/n2\pi k/n2πk/n and ω=e2πi/n\omega=e^{2\pi\ii/n}ω=e2πi/n, the maps k ⟼ ρk ⟼ ωkk \,\longmapsto\, \rho_k \,\longmapsto\, \omega^kk⟼ρk​⟼ωk are bijections Zn→{ρk}→{ωk}\Z_n\to\{\rho_k\}\to\{\omega^k\}Zn​→{ρk​}→{ωk} converting addition modulo nnn into composition of rotations into multiplication of complex numbers — in each case because ρaρb=ρa+b\rho_a\rho_b=\rho_{a+b}ρa​ρb​=ρa+b​ and ωaωb=ωa+b\omega^a\omega^b=\omega^{a+b}ωaωb=ωa+b, exponents modulo nnn. Example 1.22 (D3≅S3D_3\cong S_3D3​≅S3​). Every symmetry operation of the triangle permutes the vertex set {1,2,3}\{1,2,3\}{1,2,3}, so there is a map φ ⁣:D3→S3\varphi\colon D_3\to S_3φ:D3​→S3​ sending each operation to the vertex permutation it induces. Remark 1.23 (Invariants, and the classification problem). Isomorphic groups share every property expressible in terms of the group multiplication alone: the order ∣G∣\abs{G}∣G∣, abelianness, and the multiset of orders of elements, among others. Such invariants are the practical tool for proving two groups are not isomorphic: on Sheet 1 you will check that D4D_4D4​ and C8C_8C8​, both of order 888, are distinguished by their element orders. Classifying all finite groups up to isomorphism is the great organising problem of the subject; we survey what is known in Section 1.8.

Groups beyond the finite

Part I of this course concerns finite groups, but the matrix groups central to Part II cost nothing to define now, and they sharpen the picture of where the finite groups sit.

Definition 1.24 (Matrix groups). Let F\FF denote R\RR or C\CC. Symmetry groups in physics: point groups

Model a molecule by the equilibrium positions of its nuclei — a finite set of labelled points in space, the labels recording the chemical species. The point group of the molecule is the group of all orthogonal transformations about its centre of mass (rotations, reflections, and their combinations) that map the configuration onto itself, carrying each nucleus to the position of a nucleus of the same species. “Point group” records that, unlike the translational symmetries of a crystal, all these operations fix a point.

Chemists’ (Schoenflies) notation will be useful: EEE for the identity, CnC_nCn​ for a rotation through 2π/n2\pi/n2π/n — the same symbol as the cyclic group it generates — and σ\sigmaσ for a reflection plane, with a subscript vvv (vertical) when the plane contains the principal rotation axis.

Example 1.27 (Water). The water molecule H2_22​O is planar and isosceles (Figure 1.2, left). Its symmetry operations are: the identity EEE; the rotation C2C_2C2​ through π\piπ about the axis through the oxygen, bisecting the H–O–H angle; the reflection σv\sigma_vσv​ in the molecular plane; and the reflection σv′\sigma_v'σv′​ in the plane perpendicular to the molecular plane containing the axis. The point group, denoted C2vC_{2v}C2v​, therefore has order 444. Composing any two distinct nonidentity operations yields the third — for instance σvσv′=C2\sigma_v\sigma_v'=C_2σv​σv′​=C2​, since reflecting in two perpendicular planes is a rotation through π\piπ about their line of intersection — and every element squares to the identity. The group is abelian. It is not cyclic (C4C_4C4​ contains an element of order 444; here there is none), so C2v≇C4C_{2v}\not\cong C_4C2v​≅C4​: already at order 444 there is more than one group. This four-element group, the Klein four-group, reappears on Sheet 1 as the symmetry group of a rectangle and of an anisotropic oscillator.

Example 1.28 (Ammonia). The ammonia molecule NH3_33​ is a triangular pyramid: three hydrogens at the vertices of an equilateral triangle and the nitrogen on the axis above its centre (Figure 1.2, right). The symmetry operations are the identity, the rotations C3,C32C_3,C_3^2C3​,C32​ about the vertical axis, and three reflections in the vertical planes each containing the axis and one hydrogen. Note what is absent: the reflection in the plane of the hydrogens is not a symmetry (it would move the nitrogen below the plane), and likewise the π\piπ-rotations that fix an equilateral triangle. The point group, denoted C3vC_{3v}C3v​, has order 666, and sending C3↦rC_3\mapsto rC3​↦r, σv↦s\sigma_v\mapsto sσv​↦s matches its multiplication with that of D3D_3D3​: C3v  ≅  D3  ≅  S3.C_{3v}\;\cong\; D_3\;\cong\; S_3 .C3v​≅D3​≅S3​. One abstract group, three guises: the rigid motions of a triangle, the permutations of three objects, and the symmetry of a real molecule.

These examples illustrate the general procedure: enumerate the operations, close them under composition, and identify the resulting abstract group. What the group is for — how it organises the molecule’s vibrational spectrum, and in what precise sense a group is a symmetry of the dynamics rather than merely of a shape — is the business of Lectures 6 and 7, once the representation theory of Lectures 3–5 is in hand.

Subgroups

Symmetry groups come with natural subcollections: the rotations inside DnD_nDn​, the even permutations inside SnS_nSn​. These are groups in their own right, sitting inside a larger one.

Definition 1.29 (Subgroup). A subset H⊆GH\subseteq GH⊆G of a group is a subgroup, written H≤GH\leq GH≤G, if HHH is itself a group under the multiplication of GGG.

Proposition 1.30 (Subgroup test). A subset H⊆GH\subseteq GH⊆G is a subgroup if and only if

  1. H≠∅H\neq\emptysetH=∅;

  2. h,k∈Hh,k\in Hh,k∈H implies hk∈Hhk\in Hhk∈H (closure);

  3. h∈Hh\in Hh∈H implies h−1∈Hh^{-1}\in Hh−1∈H.

If GGG is finite, conditions (i) and (ii) alone suffice.

Proof. Necessity of (i)–(iii) is clear (note eH=ee_H=eeH​=e: the identity of HHH satisfies eHeH=eHe_He_H=e_HeH​eH​=eH​, and cancellation in GGG gives eH=ee_H=eeH​=e). Conversely, given (i)–(iii): associativity is inherited from GGG; picking any h∈Hh\in Hh∈H, closure and (iii) give e=hh−1∈He=hh^{-1}\in He=hh−1∈H; so HHH is a group.

Now let GGG be finite and assume only (i) and (ii). For h∈Hh\in Hh∈H, closure puts all powers h,h2,h3,…h,h^2,h^3,\dotsh,h2,h3,… in HHH; by Proposition 1.7, hhh has some finite order nnn, so hn=e∈Hh^{n}=e\in Hhn=e∈H and hn−1=h−1∈Hh^{n-1}=h^{-1}\in Hhn−1=h−1∈H, establishing (iii). (For infinite GGG the finiteness is essential: N⊂Z\N\subset\ZN⊂Z is closed under addition but is not a subgroup.) 

Example 1.31. (i) Every group has the trivial subgroup {e}\{e\}{e} and the whole group GGG itself; subgroups other than GGG are called proper. (ii) The nnn rotations {e,r,…,rn−1}\{e,r,\dots,r^{n-1}\}{e,r,…,rn−1} in DnD_nDn​ form a subgroup isomorphic to CnC_nCn​: products and inverses of rotations are rotations. (iii) For any g∈Gg\in Gg∈G, the set of all powers ⟨g⟩={gk:k∈Z}\langle g\rangle=\{g^k:k\in\Z\}⟨g⟩={gk:k∈Z} is a subgroup, the cyclic subgroup generated by ggg; if ggg has finite order then ∣⟨g⟩∣=ord⁡(g)\abs{\langle g\rangle}=\ord(g)∣⟨g⟩∣=ord(g), since the distinct powers are exactly e,g,…,gord⁡(g)−1e,g,\dots,g^{\ord(g)-1}e,g,…,gord(g)−1. (iv) The even permutations form a subgroup An≤SnA_n\leq S_nAn​≤Sn​, the alternating group: by multiplicativity of sgn⁡\sgnsgn (Proposition 1.19), products of even permutations are even, and the finite subgroup test applies. For n≥2n\geq 2n≥2 exactly half of all permutations are even, ∣An∣=n!/2\abs{A_n}=n!/2∣An​∣=n!/2: fixing a transposition τ\tauτ, the map σ↦τσ\sigma\mapsto\tau\sigmaσ↦τσ is a bijection from even permutations to odd ones. The group A4A_4A4​, of order 121212, will be a recurring character; it is the rotation group of the regular tetrahedron.

Cosets and Lagrange’s theorem

How large can a subgroup of a finite group be? The answer is strikingly rigid, and the mechanism of the proof — slicing a group into translates of a subgroup — is used constantly in everything that follows.

Definition 1.32 (Coset). Let H≤GH\leq GH≤G and g∈Gg\in Gg∈G. The left coset of HHH by ggg is the set gH={ gh:h∈H }⊆G.gH=\{\,gh : h\in H\,\}\subseteq G .gH={gh:h∈H}⊆G. (Right cosets HgHgHg are defined analogously; in Lecture 2 the comparison of the two will lead to the notion of a normal subgroup.)

Example 1.33. In G=D3G=D_3G=D3​ take H=⟨s⟩={e,s}H=\langle s\rangle=\{e,s\}H=⟨s⟩={e,s}. The three cosets are disjoint, each has two elements, and together they exhaust the six elements of D3D_3D3​. None of this is an accident.

Lemma 1.34. Let H≤GH\leq GH≤G. Then:

  1. gH=g′HgH=g'HgH=g′H if and only if g−1g′∈Hg^{-1}g'\in Hg−1g′∈H;

  2. any two left cosets are either equal or disjoint, and the cosets partition GGG;

  3. every coset has exactly ∣H∣\abs{H}∣H∣ elements (when HHH is finite).

Proof. (i) If g−1g′=h0∈Hg^{-1}g'=h_0\in Hg−1g′=h0​∈H, then g′H=gh0H=gHg'H=gh_0H=gHg′H=gh0​H=gH, since h0H=Hh_0H=Hh0​H=H (the map h↦h0hh\mapsto h_0hh↦h0​h is a bijection of HHH by cancellation and closure). Conversely if gH=g′HgH=g'HgH=g′H then g′=g′e∈g′H=gHg'=g'e\in g'H=gHg′=g′e∈g′H=gH, so g′=ghg'=ghg′=gh for some h∈Hh\in Hh∈H, i.e. g−1g′=h∈Hg^{-1}g'=h\in Hg−1g′=h∈H.

(ii) Suppose gH∩g′H≠∅gH\cap g'H\neq\emptysetgH∩g′H=∅, say gh=g′h′gh=g'h'gh=g′h′. Then g−1g′=h(h′)−1∈Hg^{-1}g'=h(h')^{-1}\in Hg−1g′=h(h′)−1∈H, so gH=g′HgH=g'HgH=g′H by (i). Since g=ge∈gHg=ge\in gHg=ge∈gH, every element lies in some coset; hence the distinct cosets partition GGG.

Theorem 1.35 (Lagrange). If GGG is a finite group and H≤GH\leq GH≤G, then ∣H∣\abs{H}∣H∣ divides ∣G∣\abs{G}∣G∣. More precisely, if [G:H][G:H][G:H] denotes the number of distinct left cosets of HHH in GGG — the index of HHH — then ∣G∣=[G:H]⋅∣H∣.\abs{G}=[G:H]\cdot\abs{H}.∣G∣=[G:H]⋅∣H∣.

Proof. By Lemma 1.34, GGG is partitioned into [G:H][G:H][G:H] cosets, each of size exactly ∣H∣\abs{H}∣H∣. 

Corollary 1.36. Let GGG be a finite group. Then:

  1. ord⁡(g)\ord(g)ord(g) divides ∣G∣\abs{G}∣G∣ for every g∈Gg\in Gg∈G;

  2. g∣G∣=eg^{\abs{G}}=eg∣G∣=e for every g∈Gg\in Gg∈G;

  3. if ∣G∣=p\abs{G}=p∣G∣=p is prime, then G≅CpG\cong C_pG≅Cp​; in particular GGG is cyclic and abelian.

Proof. (i) ord⁡(g)=∣⟨g⟩∣\ord(g)=\abs{\langle g\rangle}ord(g)=∣⟨g⟩∣, which divides ∣G∣\abs{G}∣G∣ by Lagrange. (ii) Write ∣G∣=ord⁡(g) m\abs{G}=\ord(g)\,m∣G∣=ord(g)m; then g∣G∣=(gord⁡(g))m=eg^{\abs{G}}=(g^{\ord(g)})^m=eg∣G∣=(gord(g))m=e. (iii) Pick g≠eg\neq eg=e. Part (ii) is the group-theoretic engine behind Fermat’s little theorem and Euler’s theorem in number theory. For us, Lagrange’s theorem is above all a search-space killer: it dramatically restricts what subgroups, element orders, and (from Lecture 4 onwards) dimensions of irreducible representations are possible.

Example 1.37 (All subgroups of D3D_3D3​). A subgroup of D3D_3D3​ has order 111, 222, 333 or 666. Order 111: only {e}\{e\}{e}. Remark 1.38. The converse of Lagrange’s theorem is false: a divisor of ∣G∣\abs{G}∣G∣ need not be the order of any subgroup. The smallest counterexample is the alternating group A4A_4A4​, of order 121212, which has no subgroup of order 666. Partial converses exist — notably Cauchy’s theorem, that a prime divisor of ∣G∣\abs{G}∣G∣ is always the order of some element — and the case p=2p=2p=2 appears as a guided exercise on Sheet 1.

A deeper organising principle, developed in Lecture 2, is that finite groups are built from simple groups — those admitting no nontrivial quotients — somewhat as integers are built from primes and molecules from atoms. The classification of the finite simple groups, completed in the late twentieth century after tens of thousands of journal pages, is among the largest collaborative achievements in mathematics; its strangest entry, the Monster group, with about 8×10538\times 10^{53}8×1053 elements, has unexpected connections to string theory. None of this machinery is needed in this course, but it is well to know the shape of the country whose border region we now begin to survey: in Lecture 2 we study how groups map to one another and decompose, and from Lecture 3 the central theme becomes how groups act on linear spaces — which is precisely the form in which symmetry enters quantum mechanics.

Summary

  • Group. A set with an associative product, an identity, and inverses (Definition 1.1); abelian when the product commutes.
  • Standard families. The cyclic groups, the dihedral group DnD_nDn​ of order 2n2n2n (symmetries of the nnn-gon, Proposition 1.10), and the symmetric group SnS_nSn​ with its sign homomorphism (Definition 1.18).
  • Isomorphism. A structure-preserving bijection; groups are studied up to isomorphism (Definition 1.20).
  • Subgroups and cosets. The subgroup test (Proposition 1.30); cosets partition a group into blocks of equal size (Lemma 1.34).
  • Lagrange. ∣H∣\lvert H\rvert∣H∣ divides ∣G∣\lvert G\rvert∣G∣ for H≤GH\le GH≤G (Theorem 1.35); hence element orders divide ∣G∣\lvert G\rvert∣G∣ and groups of prime order are cyclic (Corollary 1.36).

Self-check

5 questions on the ideas this lecture is most often misread on. Answer before you look — a wrong answer you thought about is worth more than a right one you guessed.

1Which property says that multiplying two elements of G produces another element of G?

The operation must close on the underlying set.

Closure means that the group product is a map G × G → G.

2How many elements does the symmetry group D₃ of an equilateral triangle have?

Count the rotations and the reflections separately.

D₃ contains three rotations and three reflections, hence six elements.

3What does rσ ≠ σr demonstrate about D₃?

An abelian group has the same product in either order.

The unequal products exhibit non-commutativity, so D₃ is non-abelian.

4Which number cannot be the order of a subgroup of a group of order 12?

Use divisibility, not only inequality.

Lagrange's theorem requires a subgroup order to divide 12; 5 does not.

5What follows for every group of prime order?

Apply Lagrange's theorem to a non-identity element.

A non-identity element must have order equal to the prime, so it generates the group.
