Lecture 10

SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) and su(2)\su(2)su(2): highest-weight theory and spin

What this lecture does

Lecture 9 ended with a licence: over SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2), group representations and algebra representations are the same thing. This lecture spends it. We set up the group and its algebra in the two standard bases and legalise complexification; we prove the classification theorem — exactly one irreducible representation of each dimension 2j+12j+12j+1, with JzJ_zJz​-spectrum {−j,…,j}\{-j,\dots,j\}{−j,…,j} and ladder coefficients derived rather than decreed; we realise every spin jjj concretely on homogeneous polynomials, complete with an explicit invariant inner product; we show that the Casimir operator equals j(j+1)j(j+1)j(j+1); and we read the result back into physics, where it is the entire nonrelativistic theory of angular momentum and spin.

The group, the algebra, and the two dictionaries

The calculation this lecture performs — complexify, find a highest weight, descend by ladders, count — is the model for every representation theory Part II will do. We first lay out the cast.

Lemma 10.3 (Complexification is legal). Let VVV be a finite-dimensional complex vector space. Every representation π\piπ of the real Lie algebra su(2)\su(2)su(2) on VVV extends uniquely to a complex-linear representation πC\pi_{\C}πC​ of sl(2,C)=su(2)⊕i su(2)\sllie(2,\C)=\su(2)\oplus\ii\,\su(2)sl(2,C)=su(2)⊕isu(2), namely πC(X+iY)=π(X)+i π(Y),X,Y∈su(2),\pi_{\C}(X+\ii Y)=\pi(X)+\ii\,\pi(Y), \qquad X,Y\in\su(2),πC​(X+iY)=π(X)+iπ(Y),X,Y∈su(2), and every complex-linear representation of sl(2,C)\sllie(2,\C)sl(2,C) arises this way. A subspace is π\piπ-invariant if and only if it is πC\pi_{\C}πC​-invariant; an operator intertwines two representations of su(2)\su(2)su(2) if and only if it intertwines their extensions. Proof. The formula is well defined because su(2)∩i su(2)={0}\su(2)\cap\ii\,\su(2)=\{0\}su(2)∩isu(2)={0} (Lecture 8), and it is the only complex-linear extension. Conversely, restricting a complex-linear representation of sl(2,C)\sllie(2,\C)sl(2,C) to the real subalgebra su(2)\su(2)su(2) inverts the construction. The images π(su(2))\pi(\su(2))π(su(2)) and πC(sl(2,C))\pi_{\C}(\sllie(2,\C))πC​(sl(2,C)) have the same complex span, which proves the statements about invariant subspaces and intertwiners. 

This is why complexifying costs nothing and buys everything: the representations are the same, but sl(2,C)\sllie(2,\C)sl(2,C) owns the ladder operators, and C\CC — being algebraically closed — owns eigenvectors. The classification can begin.

The Casimir operator and complete reducibility

Part I’s sharpest small tool was Schur’s lemma. Its algebra edition costs two lines.

Lemma 10.10 (Schur’s lemma, algebra edition). Let π\piπ be an irreducible complex-linear representation of a Lie algebra on VVV, and let T∈gl(V)T\in\gllie(V)T∈gl(V) commute with every π(X)\pi(X)π(X). Then T=c 1T=c\,\mathbb 1T=c1 for some c∈Cc\in\Cc∈C.

Proof. TTT has an eigenvalue ccc, and ker⁡(T−c 1)\ker(T-c\,\mathbb 1)ker(T−c1) is nonzero and invariant under every π(X)\pi(X)π(X), hence equals VVV. (Lecture 3’s proof; only the cast has changed.) 

Now a new character. Products such as Jx2J_x^{2}Jx2​ are not elements of the Lie algebra — the abstract algebra knows only brackets — but any representation embeds the players in an associative algebra of operators, where products make sense. (The abstract home for such products, the universal enveloping algebra, is further reading; we will not need it.) Define, in any complex-linear representation of sl(2,C)\sllie(2,\C)sl(2,C), the Casimir operator J2=Jx2+Jy2+Jz2.J^{2}=J_x^{2}+J_y^{2}+J_z^{2}.J2=Jx2​+Jy2​+Jz2​.

What about reducible representations? Part I’s answer survives in two instalments, one payable now.

Theorem 10.12 (Complete reducibility).

  1. Every finite-dimensional unitary representation of any matrix Lie group is an orthogonal direct sum of irreducible subrepresentations.

  2. (Stated; proved in Lecture 12.) Every representation of SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) on a finite-dimensional complex vector space admits an invariant inner product.

Consequently every finite-dimensional representation of SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) is equivalent to a direct sum of spin representations Πj\Pi_jΠj​.

Proof of (i). If VVV is not already irreducible, choose a proper nonzero invariant WWW; for v∈W⊥v\in W^{\perp}v∈W⊥ and w∈Ww\in Ww∈W, ⟨Π(g)v,w⟩=⟨v,Π(g)†w⟩=⟨v,Π(g−1)w⟩=0\inner{\Pi(g)v}{w}=\inner{v}{\Pi(g)^{\dagger}w} =\inner{v}{\Pi(g^{-1})w}=0⟨Π(g)v,w⟩=⟨v,Π(g)†w⟩=⟨v,Π(g−1)w⟩=0, so W⊥W^{\perp}W⊥ is invariant too, and induction on dimension finishes — Lecture 3’s argument, unchanged. 

Statement (ii) is Part I’s averaging trick with the sum over the group replaced by an integral over SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2); constructing that integral — the Haar measure — is Lecture 12’s opening business, where Maschke’s theorem returns in continuous dress. (A purely algebraic proof of complete reducibility also exists, with the Casimir doing the splitting; it is starred further reading.) Via Theorem 10.4 and Lemma 10.3, the same decomposition holds for representations of su(2)\su(2)su(2) and complex-linear representations of sl(2,C)\sllie(2,\C)sl(2,C).

The quantum theory of angular momentum and spin

A quantum system is rotationally symmetric when rotations act on its Hilbert space by unitaries commuting with the Hamiltonian — Lecture 9’s quantum Noether setting. Acting by which group? The geometric answer is SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3); the group this lecture has classified is SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2). The discrepancy is real, productive, and deliberately deferred: quantum mechanics cares only about rays, and Lecture 11 will prove that ray-level representations of SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3) are precisely ordinary representations of SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2). For now we record the IOU and take the symmetry group to be SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2), noting that at the level of observables there is nothing to choose: su(2)≅so(3)\su(2)\cong\so(3)su(2)≅so(3) (Lectures 8 and 9), and the Hermitian generators obey [Ja,Jb]=i ϵabcJc[J_a,J_b]=\ii\,\epsilon_{abc}J_c[Ja​,Jb​]=iϵabc​Jc​ either way.

Dividend one: multiplets. If [H,Π(g)]=0[H,\Pi(g)]=0[H,Π(g)]=0, every energy eigenspace carries a representation of SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) (Lecture 9), which by Theorem 10.12 splits into spin-jjj irreducibles. Energy levels therefore come in multiplets of dimension 2j+1=1,2,3,…2j+1=1,2,3,\dots2j+1=1,2,3,…; within a multiplet, J2J^{2}J2 and JzJ_zJz​ commute with each other and with HHH, and the basis adapted to all three is the ∣j,m⟩\ket{j,m}∣j,m⟩ of Proposition 10.6. The entire ∣j,m⟩\ket{j,m}∣j,m⟩ bookkeeping of quantum mechanics — which states exist, what J±J_{\pm}J±​ do to them, why degeneracies are 2j+12j+12j+1 — is Theorem 10.4 read aloud as physics.

Dividend two: spin. Lecture 9 proved that orbital angular momentum is integer-valued: the function action on wavefunctions ψ(x)\psi(x)ψ(x) represents a U(1)\U(1)U(1) along each axis, and the circle forces m∈Zm\in\Zm∈Z. The classification now exhibits what else is on offer: representations with half-integer jjj, unreachable by functions on space, perfectly available on an internal factor. A particle of spin sss carries H=L2(R3)⊗C2s+1,\mathcal H=L^{2}(\R^{3})\otimes\C^{2s+1},H=L2(R3)⊗C2s+1, rotations acting on the first factor by the function action and on the second by Πs\Pi_sΠs​. This is the half-integers’ home promised in Lecture 9. Why nature is permitted to occupy it — the address, so to speak — is still Lecture 11’s.

Example 10.15 (Stern–Gerlach: counting a dimension). Send a beam of atoms through a strongly inhomogeneous field. The force on a moment is proportional to mmm, the JzJ_zJz​ eigenvalue, so the beam splits into one spot per eigenvalue: 2j+12j+12j+1 spots. Silver atoms give two. For integer jjj the full turn acts trivially, and all is calm. For half-integer jjj it acts as −1-\mathbb 1−1: rotating a spinor by 2π2\pi2π flips its sign, and only a 4π4\pi4π turn restores it. Two reactions are available. The alarmed one — a 2π2\pi2π rotation does nothing to space, so an operator that does something cannot represent rotations — is correct, and its resolution is that Π1/2\Pi_{1/2}Π1/2​ represents not SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3) but SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2), where the path of exponentials closes only after 4π4\pi4π. The relaxed one — a global sign is invisible on a single ray, so quantum mechanics may not care — is also correct, but incomplete: put the rotated spinor in superposition with an unrotated companion and the sign interferes observably. (Neutron interferometers have measured exactly this; Lecture 11 reports the experiment.) Whether the relaxed reaction can be promoted to a principle — which “representations up to phase” quantum mechanics really permits, and why SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) is the lawful answer to SO(3)\SO(3)SO(3)’s question — is precisely Lecture 11’s business.
