Lecture 1

Groups, examples, and the landscape of finite groups

What this lecture does

We abstract the group axioms from the behaviour of symmetry operations, assemble a working zoo of finite groups — cyclic, dihedral and symmetric — together with a first glimpse of the matrix groups of Part II, make precise when two groups are “the same”, and prove the fundamental counting theorem of the subject, Lagrange’s theorem.

From symmetry operations to the group axioms

Symmetry, in physics as in geometry, is always symmetry under something: a transformation that leaves the relevant structure unchanged. The structure may be a geometric figure, a crystal lattice, a molecule’s equilibrium configuration, or — as we shall see at the end of Part I — the Hamiltonian of a physical system. The first task of this course is to extract, from the behaviour of such transformations, a small set of axioms, and then to develop the mathematics of those axioms far enough that it starts returning concrete physical predictions: degeneracies of energy levels, patterns of vibrational spectra, selection rules.

We begin with the simplest nontrivial example. Let Δ\DeltaΔ be an equilateral triangle in the plane, its vertices labelled 1,2,31,2,31,2,3. A symmetry operation of Δ\DeltaΔ is a distance-preserving map of the plane that carries Δ\DeltaΔ onto itself. There are exactly six (Figure 1.1):

  • the identity map eee, which moves nothing;

  • the rotations rrr and r2r^2r2 about the centroid, through 2π/32\pi/32π/3 and 4π/34\pi/34π/3 anticlockwise;

  • the reflections σ1,σ2,σ3\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3σ1​,σ2​,σ3​ in the three axes joining a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side.

Each operation is determined by what it does to the vertices. Writing g ⁣:i↦jg\colon i\mapsto jg:i↦j when ggg carries vertex iii to the position of vertex jjj. For instance, r ⁣:1↦2,  2↦3,  3↦1,σ1 ⁣:1↦1,  2↦3,  3↦2,r\colon 1\mapsto 2,\; 2\mapsto 3,\; 3\mapsto 1, \qquad \sigma_1\colon 1\mapsto 1,\; 2\mapsto 3,\; 3\mapsto 2,r:1↦2,2↦3,3↦1,σ1​:1↦1,2↦3,3↦2, and similarly for the rest.

Two operations performed in succession are again a symmetry operation. Throughout the course we write composition right to left: ghghgh means “first hhh, then ggg”. So (gh)(x)=g(h(x))(gh)(x)=g\bigl(h(x)\bigr)(gh)(x)=g(h(x)) for every point xxx.

Trace the vertices: rσ1 ⁣:1↦σ11↦r2,2↦3↦1,3↦2↦3,r\sigma_1\colon 1\xmapsto{\sigma_1}1\xmapsto{r}2,\quad 2\mapsto 3\mapsto 1,\quad 3\mapsto 2\mapsto 3,rσ1​:1σ1​​1r​2,2↦3↦1,3↦2↦3, So rσ1r\sigma_1rσ1​ fixes vertex 333 and swaps 1,21,21,2, which is σ3\sigma_3σ3​. The same computation in the other order gives σ1r=σ2\sigma_1 r=\sigma_2σ1​r=σ2​. Hence rσ1=σ3≠σ2=σ1r.r\sigma_1=\sigma_3\neq\sigma_2=\sigma_1 r.rσ1​=σ3​=σ2​=σ1​r. The order in which symmetry operations are performed matters. This noncommutativity is not a defect. It is the source of most of the structure we study in this course.

Four features of this example are general. The composition of two symmetry operations is a symmetry operation. Composition of operations is associative. The identity map is a symmetry operation. Every symmetry operation can be “undone” by an inverse operation. That is all we need in order to define symmetry abstractly.

The definition of a group

Definition 1.1 (Group). A group is a set GGG together with a map G×G→GG\times G\to GG×G→G, (g,h)↦gh(g,h)\mapsto gh(g,h)↦gh, called the group multiplication, such that:

  1. Associativity: (gh)k=g(hk)(gh)k=g(hk)(gh)k=g(hk) for all g,h,k∈Gg,h,k\in Gg,h,k∈G.

  2. Identity: there exists e∈Ge\in Ge∈G with eg=ge=geg=ge=geg=ge=g for all g∈Gg\in Gg∈G.

  3. Inverses: for every g∈Gg\in Gg∈G there exists g−1∈Gg^{-1}\in Gg−1∈G with gg−1=g−1g=egg^{-1}=g^{-1}g=egg−1=g−1g=e.

Remark 1.2 (Conventions). The property that gh∈Ggh\in Ggh∈G for all g,h∈Gg,h\in Gg,h∈G is called closure. We write the multiplication by juxtaposition and call ghghgh the product, even when the operation is composition of maps or addition of numbers. We denote the identity element eee (German einselement); the physics and chemistry literature often writes EEE, and algebra 111. By associativity, products such as ghkghkghk need no brackets. Powers gn=g⋯gg^n=g\cdots ggn=g⋯g (nnn factors), g0=eg^0=eg0=e, g−n=(g−1)ng^{-n}=(g^{-1})^ng−n=(g−1)n obey the usual rules of exponents.

Definition 1.3 (Abelian group). A group GGG is abelian (or commutative) if gh=hggh=hggh=hg for all g,h∈Gg,h\in Gg,h∈G. For abelian groups one sometimes uses additive notation: g+hg+hg+h for the product, 000 for the identity, and −g-g−g for the inverse.

Example 1.4.

  1. The integers Z\ZZ together with addition form an abelian group: 000 is the identity, and the inverse of nnn is −n-n−n. The same holds for the real and complex numbers, R\RR and C\CC, together with addition.
  2. The nonzero rationals Q∖{0}\Q\setminus\{0\}Q∖{0} together with multiplication form an abelian group, as do R∖{0}\R\setminus\{0\}R∖{0} and C∖{0}\C\setminus\{0\}C∖{0}; the two-element set {1,−1}\{1,-1\}{1,−1} together with multiplication is a group too.
  3. The natural numbers N\NN together with addition are not a group (no inverses), and Z\ZZ together with multiplication is not a group (only 111 and −1-1−1 have inverses).
  4. The six symmetry operations of the triangle together with composition form a nonabelian group; we showed this above.

  1. the identity element is unique;

  2. each g∈Gg\in Gg∈G has a unique inverse;

  3. (cancellation) gh=gkgh=gkgh=gk implies h=kh=kh=k, and hg=kghg=kghg=kg implies h=kh=kh=k;

  4. (gh)−1=h−1g−1(gh)^{-1}=h^{-1}g^{-1}(gh)−1=h−1g−1 and (g−1)−1=g(g^{-1})^{-1}=g(g−1)−1=g.

  4. In the triangle group, the rotation rrr has order 333 and each reflection σi\sigma_iσi​ has order 222: a reflection is its own inverse.

Examples

In this section we meet three families of finite groups: cyclic, dihedral and symmetric groups. These groups will be our laboratory for the first part of the course.

Cyclic groups

Definition 1.8 (Cyclic group). A group GGG is cyclic if there is an element g∈Gg\in Gg∈G, called a generator, such that every element of GGG is a power of ggg: G={ gk:k∈Z }.G=\{\,g^k : k\in\Z\,\}.G={gk:k∈Z}. A cyclic group having nnn elements is denoted CnC_nCn​.

  1. the rotations of the plane through multiples of 2π/n2\pi/n2π/n about a chosen point, under composition;

  2. the nnn-th roots of unity {1,ω,ω2,…,ωn−1}⊂C\{1,\omega,\omega^2,\dots,\omega^{n-1}\}\subset\C{1,ω,ω2,…,ωn−1}⊂C, where ω=e2πi/n\omega=e^{2\pi\ii/n}ω=e2πi/n, under multiplication;

Remark 1.12 (Generators and relations). Symmetric groups

Definition 1.13 (Symmetric group). The symmetric group SnS_nSn​ is the set of all permutations (bijections, or rearrangements) σ ⁣:{1,…,n}→{1,…,n}\sigma\colon\{1,\dots,n\}\to\{1,\dots,n\}σ:{1,…,n}→{1,…,n}, together with their composition.

Proposition 1.17. Every permutation is a product of transpositions.

Proof. By Proposition 1.15 every permutation can be written as a product of disjoint cycles. Definition 1.18 (Sign of a permutation). Comparing groups: isomorphism

The rotations through multiples of 2π/32\pi/32π/3, the cube roots of unity, and the residues {0,1,2}\{0,1,2\}{0,1,2} modulo 333 are “the same group” in three different guises. Their elements can be paired up so that the multiplication tables coincide. The following definition makes this precise.

Remark 1.24 (Invariants, and the classification problem). Isomorphic groups have all the same properties that can be expressed using the multiplication alone: the same order ∣G∣\abs{G}∣G∣, the same abelianness, the same orders of elements. Such properties are called invariants.

Invariants are the easy way to show that two groups are not isomorphic. On exercise sheet 1 you will check that the order-888 groups D4D_4D4​ and C8C_8C8​ are distinguished by their element orders — so they cannot be isomorphic. Classifying all finite groups up to isomorphism is one of the great problems of finite group theory. The reading at the end of this lecture surveys it briefly.

Part I of this course concerns finite groups, but we can define the main matrix groups of Part II already now. They show where the finite groups sit among all groups.

Remark 1.27 (Leonardo’s theorem). Every finite group of orthogonal transformations of the plane is cyclic or dihedral. This result is traditionally attributed to Leonardo da Vinci, who catalogued the possible symmetries of buildings.

For the statement to hold as written, the dihedral family must be extended downwards by two members, D1D_1D1​ and D2D_2D2​. The group D1D_1D1​ consists of the identity and one reflection, so it has order 222. The group D2D_2D2​ consists of two perpendicular reflections and the half-turn; it is the Klein four-group, which is the symmetry group of water and reappears in Lecture 5. Definition 1.9 does not name these groups, because no nnn-gon with n<3n<3n<3 realises them.

The analogous classification of symmetries in three-dimensional space is taken up in Lecture 2. It determines the possible shapes of molecules and underlies crystallography.

Subgroups

Symmetry groups offer natural subsets of their own: the rotations inside DnD_nDn​, the even permutations inside SnS_nSn​. These are groups in their own right, sitting inside a larger one.

Definition 1.28 (Subgroup). A subset H⊆GH\subseteq GH⊆G of a group is a subgroup, written H≤GH\leq GH≤G, if HHH is itself a group under the inherited multiplication.

Proposition 1.29 (Subgroup test). A subset H⊆GH\subseteq GH⊆G is a subgroup if and only if

  1. H≠∅H\neq\emptysetH=∅;

  2. h,k∈Hh,k\in Hh,k∈H implies hk∈Hhk\in Hhk∈H (closure);

  3. h∈Hh\in Hh∈H implies h−1∈Hh^{-1}\in Hh−1∈H.

If GGG is finite, conditions (i) and (ii) suffice.

Proof. The necessity of all three conditions is clear. Note only that eH=ee_H=eeH​=e: the identity of HHH satisfies eHeH=eHe_He_H=e_HeH​eH​=eH​, and cancellation in GGG gives eH=ee_H=eeH​=e.

Conversely, suppose all three hold. Associativity is inherited from GGG. Pick any h∈Hh\in Hh∈H: closure and the inverse condition give e=hh−1∈He=hh^{-1}\in He=hh−1∈H. So HHH is a group.

Let GGG be finite, and assume only the first two conditions. Take h∈Hh\in Hh∈H. Closure puts all powers h,h2,h3,…h,h^2,h^3,\dotsh,h2,h3,… in HHH. By Proposition 1.7 the element hhh has some finite order nnn, so hn=e∈Hh^{n}=e\in Hhn=e∈H and hn−1=h−1∈Hh^{n-1}=h^{-1}\in Hhn−1=h−1∈H. That is the inverse condition.

Note that the last claim holds only for finite groups. In an infinite group closure alone is not enough: the set N⊂Z\N\subset\ZN⊂Z is closed under addition, but is not a subgroup of Z\ZZ, since it has no inverses. 

Example 1.30. (i) Every group has the trivial subgroup {e}\{e\}{e} and the whole group GGG itself. Subgroups other than GGG are called proper.

(ii) The nnn rotations {e,r,…,rn−1}\{e,r,\dots,r^{n-1}\}{e,r,…,rn−1} in DnD_nDn​ form a subgroup isomorphic to CnC_nCn​, since products and inverses of rotations are rotations.

(iii) For any g∈Gg\in Gg∈G the set of all powers ⟨g⟩={gk:k∈Z}\langle g\rangle=\{g^k:k\in\Z\}⟨g⟩={gk:k∈Z} is a subgroup. It is called the cyclic subgroup generated by ggg. The group A4A_4A4​, of order 121212, will appear again and again in this course; it is the rotation group of the regular tetrahedron.

Cosets and Lagrange’s theorem

How large can a subgroup of a finite group be? The answer is strikingly rigid. The idea of the proof — slicing a group into translates of a subgroup — will be used constantly in everything that follows.

Definition 1.31 (Coset). Let H≤GH\leq GH≤G and g∈Gg\in Gg∈G. The left coset of HHH by ggg is the set gH={ gh:h∈H }⊆G.gH=\{\,gh : h\in H\,\}\subseteq G .gH={gh:h∈H}⊆G.

Right cosets HgHgHg are defined analogously. In Lecture 2 the comparison of the two will lead to the notion of a normal (invariant) subgroup.

  1. ord⁡(g)\ord(g)ord(g) divides ∣G∣\abs{G}∣G∣ for every g∈Gg\in Gg∈G;

Proof.

  1. ord⁡(g)=∣⟨g⟩∣\ord(g)=\abs{\langle g\rangle}ord(g)=∣⟨g⟩∣, which divides ∣G∣\abs{G}∣G∣ by Lagrange.

Remark 1.37. The converse of Lagrange’s theorem is false: a divisor of ∣G∣\abs{G}∣G∣ need not be the order of any subgroup. The number of groups varies very irregularly, and jumps at powers of 222. There are 141414 groups of order 161616. Of all groups of order at most 200020002000, most have order exactly 102410241024.

A deeper organising principle is developed in Lecture 2. Finite groups are built from simple groups, that is, from groups admitting no nontrivial quotients. This is somewhat as integers are built from primes, and molecules from atoms.

The classification of the finite simple groups was completed in the late twentieth century, after tens of thousands of journal pages. It is among the largest collaborative achievements in mathematics. Its strangest entry is the Monster group, with about 8×10538\times 10^{53}8×1053 elements; it has unexpected connections to string theory.

None of this is needed in this course. Still, it is worth knowing what sort of territory we are taking our first steps in. In Lecture 2 we study how groups map to one another and how they decompose. From Lecture 3 the central theme becomes how groups act on linear spaces. That is precisely the form in which symmetry enters quantum mechanics.

Summary

  • Group. A set with an associative product, an identity, and inverses (Definition 1.1); abelian when the product commutes.
  • Standard families. The cyclic groups, the dihedral group DnD_nDn​ of order 2n2n2n (symmetries of the nnn-gon, Proposition 1.10), and the symmetric group SnS_nSn​ with its multiplicative sign map (a homomorphism, in Lecture 2’s language) (Definition 1.18).
  • Isomorphism. A structure-preserving bijection; groups are studied up to isomorphism (Definition 1.21).
  • Subgroups and cosets. The subgroup test (Proposition 1.29); cosets partition a group into blocks of equal size (Lemma 1.33).
  • Lagrange. ∣H∣\lvert H\rvert∣H∣ divides ∣G∣\lvert G\rvert∣G∣ for H≤GH\le GH≤G (Theorem 1.34); hence element orders divide ∣G∣\lvert G\rvert∣G∣ and groups of prime order are cyclic (Corollary 1.35).

Self-check

5 questions on the ideas this lecture is most often misread on. Answer before you look — a wrong answer you thought about is worth more than a right one you guessed.

1Which property says that multiplying two elements of G produces another element of G?

The operation must close on the underlying set.

Closure means that the group product is a map G × G → G.

2How many elements does the symmetry group D₃ of an equilateral triangle have?

Count the rotations and the reflections separately.

D₃ contains three rotations and three reflections, hence six elements.

3What does rσ ≠ σr demonstrate about D₃?

An abelian group has the same product in either order.

The unequal products exhibit non-commutativity, so D₃ is non-abelian.

4Which number cannot be the order of a subgroup of a group of order 12?

Use divisibility, not only inequality.

Lagrange's theorem requires a subgroup order to divide 12; 5 does not.

5What follows for every group of prime order?

Apply Lagrange's theorem to a non-identity element.

A non-identity element must have order equal to the prime, so it generates the group.
