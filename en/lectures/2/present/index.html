Lecture 2

Structure of groups: homomorphisms, quotients, and products

We learn two things: to compare groups by structure-preserving maps, and to take them apart into simpler pieces. To that end we meet homomorphisms and their kernels, conjugacy and its geometric meaning, quotient groups and the first isomorphism theorem. The reading carries two further developments: how groups are “assembled” again, by direct and semidirect products, and how a finite group is decomposed into simple constituents.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Find the kernel and the image of a homomorphism.
  • Compute conjugacy classes.
  • Form quotient groups and apply the first isomorphism theorem.

Homomorphisms

Lecture 1 declared two groups “the same” when a bijection matches their multiplication tables. Far more common, and no less useful, are maps that preserve multiplication without being bijective. They compress a group onto a smaller one, retaining some structure and forgetting the rest.

On the right-hand side the same result g(x)g(x)g(x) is carried into the transformed frame by hhh. Both routes from xxx to h(g(x))h(g(x))h(g(x)) agree:

Conjugate operations are therefore the same kind of operation, differing only in the frame in which they are written. For instance a rotation through θ\thetaθ about one axis is conjugate to the rotation through θ\thetaθ about any other axis the group can reach by its action. Quantities that do not depend on the frame are therefore constant on conjugacy classes. Characters, which we meet in Lecture 5, are of this kind.

The construction carries over unchanged from finite groups to the continuous groups of Part II. The symmetry group of the plane is the Euclidean group E(2)=R2⋊O(2)\mathrm E(2)=\R^2\rtimes\Orth(2)E(2)=R2⋊O(2) (the translations are normal, acted on by rotations and reflections), and the symmetry group of relativistic spacetime is the Poincaré group R4⋊SO+(1,3)\R^{4}\rtimes\SO^{+}(1,3)R4⋊SO+(1,3) of Lecture 17 — translations normal, acted on by Lorentz transformations. Simple groups and composition series [self-study]

Quotients suggest a notion of atom: a group that cannot be coarse-grained at all.

Definition 2.29 (Simple group). A group GGG with ∣G∣>1\abs{G}>1∣G∣>1 is simple if its only normal subgroups are {e}\{e\}{e} and GGG. Equivalently, every nontrivial homomorphism out of GGG is injective.

Proposition 2.30. An abelian group is simple if and only if it is cyclic of prime order.

Proof. By Lagrange’s theorem, CpC_pCp​ (with ppp prime) has no subgroups at all besides {e}\{e\}{e} and itself. Conversely, in an abelian group every subgroup is normal, so a simple abelian GGG may have no proper nontrivial subgroup whatsoever. Pick g≠eg\neq eg=e: then ⟨g⟩=G\langle g\rangle=G⟨g⟩=G, and ggg cannot have infinite order (else ⟨g2⟩\langle g^2\rangle⟨g2⟩ would be proper and nontrivial), so GGG is cyclic of finite order nnn; and if n=abn=abn=ab with 1<a<n1<a<n1<a<n then ⟨ga⟩\langle g^a\rangle⟨ga⟩ is proper and nontrivial. Nonabelian simple groups exist and are plentiful: the alternating groups AnA_nAn​ are simple for all n≥5n\geq 5n≥5 (we shall not prove this). The smallest, A5A_5A5​ of order 606060 — the rotation group of the icosahedron — is the smallest nonabelian simple group of all. Its simplicity is, through Galois’s theory, precisely the reason no formula in radicals solves the general quintic equation.

For C12=⟨g⟩C_{12}=\langle g\rangleC12​=⟨g⟩, the chains C12⊵⟨g2⟩⊵⟨g4⟩⊵{e}andC12⊵⟨g3⟩⊵⟨g6⟩⊵{e}C_{12}\trianglerighteq\langle g^2\rangle\trianglerighteq\langle g^4\rangle\trianglerighteq\{e\} \quad\text{and}\quad C_{12}\trianglerighteq\langle g^3\rangle\trianglerighteq\langle g^6\rangle\trianglerighteq\{e\}C12​⊵⟨g2⟩⊵⟨g4⟩⊵{e}andC12​⊵⟨g3⟩⊵⟨g6⟩⊵{e} are composition series with factors C2,C2,C3C_2,C_2,C_3C2​,C2​,C3​ and C3,C2,C2C_3,C_2,C_2C3​,C2​,C2​ respectively: the factors arrive in a different order, but as a multiset they agree — the “prime factorisation” 12=2⋅2⋅312=2\cdot2\cdot312=2⋅2⋅3 in group-theoretic form.

This stability is general:

Theorem 2.34 (Jordan–Hölder; statement only). Any two composition series of a finite group have the same length and the same composition factors with the same multiplicities, up to reordering and isomorphism.

Finite groups are thus built from simple groups as molecules from atoms, and the classification of finite simple groups mentioned in Lecture 1 is, as it were, the periodic table. But the analogy has a sting: knowing the atoms does not determine the molecule. C4C_4C4​ and the Klein four-group both have composition factors C2,C2C_2,C_2C2​,C2​, yet are not isomorphic — assembling a group from given factors (the extension problem) has in general many inequivalent solutions, and no complete classification of all finite groups exists or is expected.

Summary

  • Homomorphism. A product-preserving map; its kernel is normal and its image a subgroup (Definition 2.1, Proposition 2.3).
  • Conjugacy. Conjugacy classes partition the group; in SnS_nSn​ they are the cycle types (Proposition 2.9), and class sizes divide ∣G∣\lvert G\rvert∣G∣ (Proposition 2.12).
  • Quotients. A normal subgroup yields a quotient group (Theorem 2.17); the first isomorphism theorem gives G/ker⁡φ≅im⁡φG/\ker\varphi\cong\im\varphiG/kerφ≅imφ (Theorem 2.19).
  • Building groups. The reading puts groups back together — direct products combine independent factors, and semidirect products N⋊φHN\rtimes_\varphi HN⋊φ​H encode an action φ ⁣:H→Aut⁡(N)\varphi\colon H\to\Aut(N)φ:H→Aut(N) — and takes a finite group apart into its simple constituents.
