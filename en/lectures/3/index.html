Lecture 3

Linear representations and complete reducibility

What this lecture does

Symmetry enters physics through linear spaces: quantum states superpose, small oscillations add. This lecture sets up the central notion of the course — a linear representation of a group — together with the construction by which symmetries act on functions, and proves the two structural theorems that make finite group representations tractable: every representation can be made unitary by averaging, and every representation decomposes into irreducible pieces (Maschke’s theorem). The permutation representation of S3S_3S3​ is decomposed in full as a model computation.

Representations: definition and first examples

Lectures 1 and 2 studied groups in the abstract. Physics, however, meets a group through its action on the states of a system, and the state spaces of physics — quantum Hilbert spaces, spaces of displacements of atoms, spaces of fields — are vector spaces. The operations of the group must respect that linear structure: a symmetry operation applied to a superposition is the superposition of the transformed states. The mathematical object capturing this is the following.

Definition 3.1 (Representation). A (linear) representation of a group GGG on a finite-dimensional complex vector space VVV is a homomorphism ρ ⁣:G⟶GL(V),\rho\colon G\longrightarrow \GL(V),ρ:G⟶GL(V), where GL(V)\GL(V)GL(V) is the group of invertible linear operators on VVV. Thus to each g∈Gg\in Gg∈G is assigned an invertible operator ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g), with ρ(gh)=ρ(g) ρ(h)for all g,h∈G,\rho(gh)=\rho(g)\,\rho(h) \quad\text{for all }g,h\in G,ρ(gh)=ρ(g)ρ(h)for all g,h∈G, whence automatically ρ(e)=id⁡V\rho(e)=\id_Vρ(e)=idV​ and ρ(g−1)=ρ(g)−1\rho(g^{-1})=\rho(g)^{-1}ρ(g−1)=ρ(g)−1. The dimension (or degree) of the representation is dim⁡V\dim VdimV. We speak of “the representation (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V)”, or simply of VVV when ρ\rhoρ is understood.

Unless stated otherwise our vector spaces are complex and finite-dimensional; the real case will be flagged when it matters (and it will matter — see Example 3.13).

Remark 3.2 (Scope for this part of the course). This standing definition is the first rung of the representation-scope ladder in the student syllabus: algebraic finite-dimensional representations. Later in this lecture we also construct representations on function spaces, which may be infinite-dimensional, but Maschke’s theorem, finite character sums, trace identities, and dimension counts are used only under their stated finite-group and finite-dimensional hypotheses. Part II separately adds continuity, unitarity, projective actions, and domains of unbounded derived generators; those are not hidden in Definition 3.1.

Notation: operators versus matrices. Throughout these notes we keep two parallel notations, and the distinction is deliberate. The symbol ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) always denotes the abstract linear operator on VVV, defined without reference to any basis. Once a basis (e1,…,en)(e_1,\dots,e_n)(e1​,…,en​) of VVV is chosen, the corresponding matrices are denoted D(g)D(g)D(g) and defined by ρ(g) ej=∑i=1nDij(g) ei,\rho(g)\,e_j=\sum_{i=1}^{n} D_{ij}(g)\,e_i ,ρ(g)ej​=i=1∑n​Dij​(g)ei​, so that the jjj-th column of D(g)D(g)D(g) lists the components of the image of the jjj-th basis vector. The homomorphism property becomes matrix multiplication, D(gh)=D(g)D(h)D(gh)=D(g)D(h)D(gh)=D(g)D(h), and a map D ⁣:G→GL(n,C)D\colon G\to\GL(n,\C)D:G→GL(n,C) with this property is called a matrix representation. Irreducible representations, once we have them, will carry labels ρ(i)\rho^{(i)}ρ(i), D(i)D^{(i)}D(i). Keeping operators and matrices typographically apart costs little and prevents a great deal of confusion when several bases are in play at once — as they constantly are from Section 3.6 onwards.

How symmetries act on functions

We now come to the single most important construction of the course. The states of physical systems are very often functions on the space the symmetry group transforms: wavefunctions on configuration space, displacement fields on a molecule, temperature distributions on a body. The group’s action on points then induces an action on the functions — and it does so by a formula whose one subtle feature, an inverse, repays careful attention.

Theorem 3.9 (The function-space representation). Let the group GGG act on a set XXX, and let F(X)\mathcal F(X)F(X) denote the vector space of complex-valued functions on XXX. For g∈Gg\in Gg∈G and ψ∈F(X)\psi\in\mathcal F(X)ψ∈F(X) define (ρ(g)ψ)(x):=ψ(g−1⋅x).\bigl(\rho(g)\psi\bigr)(x) := \psi\bigl(g^{-1}\cdot x\bigr).(ρ(g)ψ)(x):=ψ(g−1⋅x). Then each ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) is an invertible linear operator and ρ ⁣:G→GL(F(X))\rho\colon G\to\GL(\mathcal F(X))ρ:G→GL(F(X)) is a representation. The same formula defines a representation on any subspace of F(X)\mathcal F(X)F(X) stable under all the ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) (for infinite XXX one works with such subspaces — polynomials, smooth functions, square-integrable functions — as the context requires).

Proof. Linearity in ψ\psiψ is clear, and ρ(e)=id⁡\rho(e)=\idρ(e)=id. The homomorphism property is a computation worth doing once and remembering forever: (ρ(g)ρ(h)ψ)(x)=(ρ(h)ψ)(g−1⋅x)=ψ(h−1⋅(g−1⋅x))=ψ((gh)−1⋅x)=(ρ(gh)ψ)(x),\bigl(\rho(g)\rho(h)\psi\bigr)(x) =\bigl(\rho(h)\psi\bigr)(g^{-1}\cdot x) =\psi\bigl(h^{-1}\cdot(g^{-1}\cdot x)\bigr) =\psi\bigl((gh)^{-1}\cdot x\bigr) =\bigl(\rho(gh)\psi\bigr)(x),(ρ(g)ρ(h)ψ)(x)=(ρ(h)ψ)(g−1⋅x)=ψ(h−1⋅(g−1⋅x))=ψ((gh)−1⋅x)=(ρ(gh)ψ)(x), using h−1g−1=(gh)−1h^{-1}g^{-1}=(gh)^{-1}h−1g−1=(gh)−1 from Lecture 1. Invertibility follows: ρ(g)ρ(g−1)=ρ(e)=id⁡\rho(g)\rho(g^{-1})=\rho(e)=\idρ(g)ρ(g−1)=ρ(e)=id. 

Why the inverse? First the conceptual answer: ρ(g)ψ\rho(g)\psiρ(g)ψ is the function ψ\psiψ carried along by ggg. Evaluating the definition at a transformed point, (ρ(g)ψ)(g⋅x)=ψ(x):\bigl(\rho(g)\psi\bigr)(g\cdot x)=\psi(x):(ρ(g)ψ)(g⋅x)=ψ(x): the transported function takes, at the transported point, the value the original function took at the original point — rotate a temperature distribution by ggg and the hot spot moves with the rotation. Second, the mechanical answer: the inverse is forced by the order of composition. Had we defined (σ(g)ψ)(x)=ψ(g⋅x)(\sigma(g)\psi)(x)=\psi(g\cdot x)(σ(g)ψ)(x)=ψ(g⋅x), the same computation would give σ(g)σ(h)=σ(hg)\sigma(g)\sigma(h)=\sigma(hg)σ(g)σ(h)=σ(hg) — the products in the wrong order, an anti-homomorphism. The inverse undoes the reversal. Lecture 1’s polynomial action is the same mechanism written in coordinate slots. With the left action σ⋅(x1,…,xn)=(xσ−1(1),…,xσ−1(n))\sigma\cdot(x_1,\dots,x_n) =(x_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma^{-1}(n)})σ⋅(x1​,…,xn​)=(xσ−1(1)​,…,xσ−1(n)​) (an action because the iii-th entry of σ⋅(τ⋅x)\sigma\cdot(\tau\cdot x)σ⋅(τ⋅x) is xτ−1σ−1(i)=x(στ)−1(i)x_{\tau^{-1}\sigma^{-1}(i)}=x_{(\sigma\tau)^{-1}(i)}xτ−1σ−1(i)​=x(στ)−1(i)​), its inverse sends xxx to (xσ(1),…,xσ(n))(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)})(xσ(1)​,…,xσ(n)​), so (Pσf)(x1,…,xn)=f(xσ(1),…,xσ(n)).(P_\sigma f)(x_1,\dots,x_n) =f(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)}).(Pσ​f)(x1​,…,xn​)=f(xσ(1)​,…,xσ(n)​). The dual representation

For a general (not necessarily orthogonal) representation, the computation above does not return the same matrices, and what it does return deserves its own name. Recall that the dual space V∗V^{*}V∗ of VVV is the space of linear maps φ ⁣:V→C\varphi\colon V\to\Cφ:V→C (functionals); given a basis (ei)(e_i)(ei​) of VVV, the dual basis (ei)(e^i)(ei) of V∗V^*V∗ is defined by ei(ej)=δije^i(e_j)=\delta_{ij}ei(ej​)=δij​. Linear functionals are functions on VVV, so the function-space construction applies to them, and it preserves linearity:

Proposition 3.11 (Dual representation). Let (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V) be a representation. Then (ρ∗(g)φ)(v):=φ(ρ(g−1)v),φ∈V∗, v∈V,\bigl(\rho^{*}(g)\varphi\bigr)(v):=\varphi\bigl(\rho(g^{-1})v\bigr), \qquad \varphi\in V^{*},\ v\in V,(ρ∗(g)φ)(v):=φ(ρ(g−1)v),φ∈V∗, v∈V, defines a representation (ρ∗,V∗)(\rho^{*},V^{*})(ρ∗,V∗), the dual (or contragredient) representation. In the dual basis its matrices are D∗(g)=D(g−1)T=(D(g)−1)T.D^{*}(g)=D(g^{-1})^{\mathsf T}=\bigl(D(g)^{-1}\bigr)^{\mathsf T}.D∗(g)=D(g−1)T=(D(g)−1)T.

Proof. Each ρ∗(g)\rho^{*}(g)ρ∗(g) maps functionals to functionals linearly, and the homomorphism property is Theorem 3.9 applied to the action of GGG on the set VVV via ρ\rhoρ. For the matrices: (ρ∗(g)ej)(ei)=ej(ρ(g−1)ei)=Dji(g−1)\bigl(\rho^{*}(g)e^j\bigr)(e_i)=e^j\bigl(\rho(g^{-1})e_i\bigr) =D_{ji}(g^{-1})(ρ∗(g)ej)(ei​)=ej(ρ(g−1)ei​)=Dji​(g−1), so expanding ρ∗(g)ej\rho^{*}(g)e^jρ∗(g)ej in the dual basis gives coefficients Dij∗(g)=Dji(g−1)D^{*}_{ij}(g)=D_{ji}(g^{-1})Dij∗​(g)=Dji​(g−1), which is the stated matrix. One can also verify directly that g↦D(g−1)Tg\mapsto D(g^{-1})^{\mathsf T}g↦D(g−1)T is a homomorphism: transposition and inversion each reverse the order of products, and two reversals cancel. 

If the matrices D(g)D(g)D(g) are unitary — and for a finite group the next section shows this can always be arranged — then D(g−1)=D(g)†D(g^{-1})=D(g)^{\dagger}D(g−1)=D(g)† and D∗(g)=D(g)‾D^{*}(g)=\overline{D(g)}D∗(g)=D(g)​: the dual representation is the complex conjugate representation. The physics reader has met this contragredient behaviour before: if kets transform as ∣ψ⟩↦ρ(g)∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}\mapsto\rho(g)\ket\psi∣ψ⟩↦ρ(g)∣ψ⟩, then bras transform with the conjugate matrices so that ⟨φ|ψ⟩\braket{\varphi}{\psi}⟨φ∣ψ⟩ stays invariant. The distinction between a representation and its conjugate, invisible when the matrices are real, prepares an important distinction in later SU(3)\SU(3)SU(3) study: the quark triplet 3\rep{3}3 and the antiquark antitriplet 3ˉ\bar{\rep{3}}3ˉ are conjugate representations. This course builds the dual/conjugate vocabulary but does not teach that particle-physics application; Jones, Ch. 8, §§8.3–8.4 is a further-reading destination.

The function-space construction is fully general, and we shall lean on it repeatedly. It is how a finite group acts on polynomials (the sign of a permutation, Lecture 1, and Example 3.10); how a point group acts on the displacement patterns of a vibrating molecule (Lecture 6); how a symmetry group acts on quantum wavefunctions, ψ(r⃗ )↦ψ(g−1r⃗ )\psi(\vec r\,)\mapsto\psi(g^{-1}\vec r\,)ψ(r)↦ψ(g−1r), which is the formula underlying degeneracies and selection rules (Lecture 7); and how the rotation group acts on functions on the sphere, whose stable subspaces are spanned by the spherical harmonics (Part II). One formula, the whole course.

Equivalence of representations

When are two representations “the same”? As with groups themselves (Lecture 1), the answer is: when a structure-preserving dictionary translates one into the other.

Definition 3.12 (Equivalence). Representations (ρV,V)(\rho_V,V)(ρV​,V) and (ρW,W)(\rho_W,W)(ρW​,W) of the same group GGG are equivalent, written ρV≅ρW\rho_V\cong\rho_WρV​≅ρW​, if there is an invertible linear map T ⁣:V→WT\colon V\to WT:V→W with T ρV(g)=ρW(g) Tfor all g∈G.T\,\rho_V(g)=\rho_W(g)\,T \qquad\text{for all }g\in G .TρV​(g)=ρW​(g)Tfor all g∈G. Such a TTT is called an intertwiner (it lets the group action pass through it).

In matrix language: choosing bases and letting SSS be the matrix of TTT, equivalence reads DW(g)=S DV(g) S−1D_W(g)=S\,D_V(g)\,S^{-1}DW​(g)=SDV​(g)S−1 for all ggg, with one and the same SSS. In particular the matrices of a single representation in two different bases are related this way — equivalent representations are one representation written in different coordinates, and all notions we build (irreducibility, characters, multiplicities) will be invariant under equivalence.

Invariant subspaces and irreducibility

Example 3.13 displayed a representation containing smaller ones. The general notions:

Definition 3.14 (Invariant subspace; irreducibility). Let (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V) be a representation. A subspace W⊆VW\subseteq VW⊆V is invariant if ρ(g)W⊆W\rho(g)W\subseteq Wρ(g)W⊆W for all g∈Gg\in Gg∈G (equivalently ρ(g)W=W\rho(g)W=Wρ(g)W=W: apply the condition to g−1g^{-1}g−1 as well). An invariant subspace carries the subrepresentation ρ∣W ⁣:g↦ρ(g)∣W\rho|_W\colon g\mapsto\rho(g)|_Wρ∣W​:g↦ρ(g)∣W​. The representation is irreducible if dim⁡V≥1\dim V\geq 1dimV≥1 and its only invariant subspaces are {0}\{0\}{0} and VVV; otherwise it is reducible. Every one-dimensional representation is irreducible.

Example 3.15 (Inside the permutation representation). In the permutation representation of SnS_nSn​ on Cn\C^nCn (Example 3.7), the line L=C u0,u0=e1+e2+⋯+en,L=\C\,u_0, \qquad u_0=e_1+e_2+\dots+e_n,L=Cu0​,u0​=e1​+e2​+⋯+en​, is invariant: permuting the summands leaves u0u_0u0​ fixed, so LLL carries a copy of the trivial representation. A second invariant subspace is V0={ v∈Cn:∑ivi=0 },V_0=\Bigl\{\,v\in\C^n : \textstyle\sum_i v_i=0\,\Bigr\},V0​={v∈Cn:∑i​vi​=0}, of dimension n−1n-1n−1: permuting the components of vvv does not change their sum. So the permutation representation is reducible for every n≥2n\geq 2n≥2, and Cn=L⊕V0\C^n=L\oplus V_0Cn=L⊕V0​ as vector spaces, both summands invariant. The subrepresentation on V0V_0V0​ is called the standard representation of SnS_nSn​; for n=3n=3n=3 we prove it irreducible in Section 3.7.

In the example, the invariant subspace came with an invariant complement. That this is automatic for finite groups is the content of Maschke’s theorem — and it is genuinely a theorem, not an algebraic triviality:

Example 3.16 (Reducible but indecomposable). Let the infinite group (Z,+)(\Z,+)(Z,+) be represented on C2\C^2C2 by ρ(n)=(1n01),ρ(n)ρ(m)=(1n+m01)=ρ(n+m).\rho(n)= \begin{pmatrix} 1&n\\ 0&1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \rho(n)\rho(m)= \begin{pmatrix} 1&n+m\\ 0&1 \end{pmatrix}=\rho(n+m).ρ(n)=(10​n1​),ρ(n)ρ(m)=(10​n+m1​)=ρ(n+m). The line Ce1\C e_1Ce1​ is invariant, so the representation is reducible. But no complementary invariant line exists: any line other than Ce1\C e_1Ce1​ is spanned by some (a1)\binom{a}{1}(1a​), and ρ(1)(a1)=(a+11)\rho(1)\binom{a}{1}=\binom{a+1}{1}ρ(1)(1a​)=(1a+1​) is never proportional to (a1)\binom{a}{1}(1a​). The representation cannot be written as a direct sum of smaller ones — the matrices can be made simultaneously triangular but never simultaneously block-diagonal. Finiteness of the group is exactly what will rule this pathology out.

Unitarity: the averaging trick

Equip VVV with a Hermitian inner product ⟨⋅,⋅⟩\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}⟨⋅,⋅⟩, conjugate-linear in the first argument (the physics convention). An operator UUU is unitary if ⟨Uu,Uv⟩=⟨u,v⟩\inner{Uu}{Uv}=\inner{u}{v}⟨Uu,Uv⟩=⟨u,v⟩ for all u,vu,vu,v, and a representation is unitary (with respect to the given product) if every ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) is.

Theorem 3.17 (Unitarisability). Let (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V) be a representation of a finite group GGG, and let ⟨⋅,⋅⟩0\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}_0⟨⋅,⋅⟩0​ be any inner product on VVV. Then ⟨u,v⟩  :=  1∣G∣∑h∈G⟨ρ(h)u, ρ(h)v⟩0\inner{u}{v}\;:=\;\frac{1}{\abs G}\sum_{h\in G} \inner{\rho(h)u}{\,\rho(h)v}_0⟨u,v⟩:=∣G∣1​h∈G∑​⟨ρ(h)u,ρ(h)v⟩0​ is an inner product on VVV with respect to which ρ\rhoρ is unitary.

Proof. Sesquilinearity is inherited term by term, and positivity holds because a sum of nonnegative terms vanishes only if each term does — in particular the h=eh=eh=e term ⟨u,u⟩0\inner{u}{u}_0⟨u,u⟩0​, so ⟨u,u⟩=0\inner{u}{u}=0⟨u,u⟩=0 forces u=0u=0u=0. For unitarity, fix ggg and compute ⟨ρ(g)u,ρ(g)v⟩=1∣G∣∑h∈G⟨ρ(hg)u,ρ(hg)v⟩0=1∣G∣∑h′∈G⟨ρ(h′)u,ρ(h′)v⟩0=⟨u,v⟩,\inner{\rho(g)u}{\rho(g)v} =\frac{1}{\abs G}\sum_{h\in G}\inner{\rho(hg)u}{\rho(hg)v}_0 =\frac{1}{\abs G}\sum_{h'\in G}\inner{\rho(h')u}{\rho(h')v}_0 =\inner{u}{v},⟨ρ(g)u,ρ(g)v⟩=∣G∣1​h∈G∑​⟨ρ(hg)u,ρ(hg)v⟩0​=∣G∣1​h′∈G∑​⟨ρ(h′)u,ρ(h′)v⟩0​=⟨u,v⟩, because as hhh runs once over GGG, so does h′=hgh'=hgh′=hg: the map h↦hgh\mapsto hgh↦hg is a bijection of GGG (cancellation — the Latin-square property of the Cayley table, Lecture 1). 

Remark 3.18 (The averaging trick). The proof’s engine — average a quantity over the group, and the average is automatically invariant because translating the summation variable merely reshuffles the sum — is the most frequently reused idea in this subject. It will prove Maschke’s theorem in a moment, the orthogonality relations in Lecture 4, and the projection formulae in Lecture 5; and in Part II, with the sum replaced by an invariant integral over a compact Lie group, the entire theory will be rebuilt on it.

In matrix terms, the theorem says every matrix representation of a finite group is equivalent to one by unitary matrices: take any basis of VVV orthonormal for the invariant product; the change of basis SSS conjugates the D(g)D(g)D(g) into unitary form.

Example 3.19 (Averaging, concretely). Let C2={e,a}C_2=\{e,a\}C2​={e,a} act on C2\C^2C2 by D(e)=1,D(a)=(110−1),D(a)2=1 ✓,D(e)=\mathbb 1, \qquad D(a)= \begin{pmatrix} 1&1\\ 0&-1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad D(a)^2=\mathbb 1\ \checkmark,D(e)=1,D(a)=(10​1−1​),D(a)2=1 ✓, a representation that is not unitary for the standard product: D(a)†D(a)=(1112)≠1D(a)^{\dagger}D(a) =\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr) \neq\mathbb 1D(a)†D(a)=(11​12​)=1. The payoff of unitarity is structural:

Corollary 3.20. In a unitary representation, the orthogonal complement of an invariant subspace is invariant.

Proof. Let WWW be invariant and w′∈W⊥w'\in W^{\perp}w′∈W⊥. For any g∈Gg\in Gg∈G and w∈Ww\in Ww∈W, ⟨ρ(g)w′, w⟩=⟨w′, ρ(g)−1w⟩=⟨w′, ρ(g−1)w⟩=0,\inner{\rho(g)w'}{\,w} =\inner{w'}{\,\rho(g)^{-1}w} =\inner{w'}{\,\rho(g^{-1})w} =0,⟨ρ(g)w′,w⟩=⟨w′,ρ(g)−1w⟩=⟨w′,ρ(g−1)w⟩=0, the first step by unitarity and the last because ρ(g−1)w∈W\rho(g^{-1})w\in Wρ(g−1)w∈W. Hence ρ(g)w′∈W⊥\rho(g)w'\in W^{\perp}ρ(g)w′∈W⊥. 

Maschke’s theorem: complete reducibility

Theorem 3.21 (Maschke). Every finite-dimensional complex representation of a finite group is a direct sum of irreducible subrepresentations: there are invariant subspaces W1,…,WkW_1,\dots,W_kW1​,…,Wk​, each irreducible, with V=W1⊕W2⊕⋯⊕Wk.V=W_1\oplus W_2\oplus\dots\oplus W_k .V=W1​⊕W2​⊕⋯⊕Wk​.

Proof. Induct on dim⁡V\dim VdimV. If VVV is irreducible (in particular if dim⁡V=1\dim V=1dimV=1), there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, equip VVV with an invariant inner product (Theorem 3.17) and let WWW be an invariant subspace with 0≠W≠V0\neq W\neq V0=W=V. By Corollary 3.20, V=W⊕W⊥V=W\oplus W^{\perp}V=W⊕W⊥ with both summands invariant and of strictly smaller dimension; apply the induction hypothesis to each. 

In matrix language: there is a single invertible SSS such that S D(g) S−1=(D(i1)(g)D(i2)(g)⋱)for every g∈GS\,D(g)\,S^{-1}= \begin{pmatrix} D^{(i_1)}(g) & & \\ & D^{(i_2)}(g) & \\ & & \ddots \end{pmatrix} \qquad\text{\emph{for every} } g\in GSD(g)S−1=​D(i1​)(g)​D(i2​)(g)​⋱​​for every g∈G simultaneously, with each diagonal block an irreducible matrix representation (Figure 3.1). The force of the statement lies in the word simultaneously: one basis, fixed once and for all, in which every group element takes block form at the same time. Finding that basis for representations of physical interest is, in a practical sense, what Lectures 5 and 6 are about.

Remark 3.22 (Notation, and what remains to prove). Collecting equivalent blocks, we write V  ≅  ⨁imi V(i),ρ  ≅  ⨁imi ρ(i),V\;\cong\;\bigoplus_i m_i\,V^{(i)}, \qquad \rho\;\cong\;\bigoplus_i m_i\,\rho^{(i)},V≅i⨁​mi​V(i),ρ≅i⨁​mi​ρ(i), where V(i)V^{(i)}V(i) runs over the inequivalent irreducible representations of GGG and the integer mi≥0m_i\geq0mi​≥0 is the multiplicity of V(i)V^{(i)}V(i) in VVV. We reserve ni=dim⁡V(i)n_i=\dim V^{(i)}ni​=dimV(i) for the irreducible dimension, so the total-dimension check is dim⁡V=∑imini\dim V=\sum_i m_i n_idimV=∑i​mi​ni​. Two caveats keep us honest. First, the decomposition is into subspaces that are not unique (already two copies of the trivial representation can be resliced into different pairs of invariant lines); what will turn out to be unique is the multiset of blocks — the multiplicities mim_imi​. Second, nothing yet tells us how many irreducibles a given group has, or how to find the mim_imi​ without hunting for invariant subspaces by hand. Lectures 4 and 5 answer all of this with startling precision: GGG has finitely many irreducibles, as many as conjugacy classes, of dimensions satisfying ∑i(dim⁡V(i))2=∣G∣\sum_i(\dim V^{(i)})^2=\abs G∑i​(dimV(i))2=∣G∣, and the multiplicities are computed by a one-line character formula.

Remark 3.23 (What the hypotheses buy). Both hypotheses of Maschke’s theorem earn their keep. Drop finiteness of the group and Example 3.16 breaks the conclusion. Drop the field assumption and it breaks too: over fields of prime characteristic ppp dividing ∣G∣\abs G∣G∣, the averaging factor 1/∣G∣1/\abs G1/∣G∣ does not exist and complete reducibility fails (the subject called modular representation theory lives in the wreckage). Over R\RR, by contrast, the proof goes through verbatim with real inner products — though, as Example 3.13 showed, the irreducible pieces over R\RR and over C\CC can differ. In Part II, compactness of the Lie group will play the role finiteness plays here.

Remark 3.24 (The geometry of the standard representation). The decomposition has a picture. One group, one irreducible two-dimensional representation, two coordinate descriptions.

Summary

  • Representation. A homomorphism G→GL(V)G\to\GL(V)G→GL(V), equivalently a linear action of GGG on VVV (Definition 3.1).
  • Function space. GGG acts on functions by (ρ(g)ψ)(x)=ψ(g−1x)(\rho(g)\psi)(x)=\psi(g^{-1}x)(ρ(g)ψ)(x)=ψ(g−1x) (Theorem 3.9), with a dual representation on the dual space (Proposition 3.11).
  • Irreducibility. An invariant subspace is one preserved by GGG; a representation is irreducible when it has no proper invariant subspace (Definition 3.14).
  • Unitarity. Every representation of a finite group is unitarisable by averaging an inner product (Theorem 3.17).
  • Maschke. Every finite-dimensional representation of a finite group is a direct sum of irreducibles (Theorem 3.21).
