Lecture 3

Linear representations

Symmetry enters physics through linear spaces: in classical physics small oscillations add, and in quantum physics states superpose. This lecture sets up the central notion of the course — a linear representation of a group — together with the construction by which symmetries act on functions, and says when two representations are the same. Its last example, the rotations of a triangle, splits into two smaller representations over C\CC but not over R\RR; splitting representations is the subject of Lecture 4.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Write down representations of a group, including permutation representations.
  • Let a group act on functions by ψ(g−1x)\psi(g^{-1}x)ψ(g−1x).
  • Decide whether two representations are equivalent.

Representations: definition and first examples

Lectures 1 and 2 studied groups in the abstract. Physics, however, meets a group through its action on the states of a system, and the state spaces of physics — quantum Hilbert spaces, spaces of displacements of atoms, spaces of fields — are vector spaces. The operations of the group must respect that linear structure: a symmetry operation applied to a superposition is the superposition of the transformed states. The mathematical object capturing this is the following.

Definition 3.1 (Representation). A (linear) representation of a group GGG on a finite-dimensional complex vector space VVV is a homomorphism ρ ⁣:G⟶GL(V),\rho\colon G\longrightarrow \GL(V),ρ:G⟶GL(V), where GL(V)\GL(V)GL(V) is the group of invertible linear operators on VVV. Thus to each g∈Gg\in Gg∈G is assigned an invertible operator ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g), with ρ(gh)=ρ(g) ρ(h)for all g,h∈G,\rho(gh)=\rho(g)\,\rho(h) \quad\text{for all }g,h\in G,ρ(gh)=ρ(g)ρ(h)for all g,h∈G, whence automatically ρ(e)=id⁡V\rho(e)=\id_Vρ(e)=idV​ and ρ(g−1)=ρ(g)−1\rho(g^{-1})=\rho(g)^{-1}ρ(g−1)=ρ(g)−1. The dimension (or degree) of the representation is dim⁡V\dim VdimV. We speak of “the representation (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V)”, or simply of VVV when ρ\rhoρ is understood.

Unless stated otherwise our vector spaces are complex and finite-dimensional; the real case will be flagged when it matters (and it will matter — see Example 3.13).

Notation: operators versus matrices. Throughout these notes we keep two parallel notations, and the distinction is deliberate. The symbol ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) always denotes the abstract linear operator on VVV, defined without reference to any basis. Once a basis (e1,…,en)(e_1,\dots,e_n)(e1​,…,en​) of VVV is chosen, the corresponding matrices are denoted D(g)D(g)D(g) and defined by ρ(g) ej=∑i=1nDij(g) ei.\rho(g)\,e_j=\sum_{i=1}^{n} D_{ij}(g)\,e_i .ρ(g)ej​=i=1∑n​Dij​(g)ei​. The jjj-th column of D(g)D(g)D(g) lists the components of the image of the jjj-th basis vector. The homomorphism property becomes matrix multiplication, D(gh)=D(g)D(h)D(gh)=D(g)D(h)D(gh)=D(g)D(h), and a map D ⁣:G→GL(n,C)D\colon G\to\GL(n,\C)D:G→GL(n,C) with this property is called a matrix representation. Keeping operators and matrices typographically apart costs little and prevents a great deal of confusion when several bases are in play at once — as they constantly are from Lecture 4 onwards.

How symmetries act on functions

We now come to the single most important construction of the course. The states of physical systems are very often functions on the space the symmetry group transforms: wavefunctions on configuration space, displacement fields on a molecule, temperature distributions on a body. The group’s action on points then induces an action on the functions — and it does so by a formula with one subtle feature: an inverse.

Theorem 3.8 (The function-space representation). Let the group GGG act on a set XXX, and let F(X)\mathcal F(X)F(X) denote the vector space of complex-valued functions on XXX. For g∈Gg\in Gg∈G and ψ∈F(X)\psi\in\mathcal F(X)ψ∈F(X) define (ρ(g)ψ)(x):=ψ(g−1⋅x).\bigl(\rho(g)\psi\bigr)(x) := \psi\bigl(g^{-1}\cdot x\bigr).(ρ(g)ψ)(x):=ψ(g−1⋅x). Then each ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) is an invertible linear operator and ρ ⁣:G→GL(F(X))\rho\colon G\to\GL(\mathcal F(X))ρ:G→GL(F(X)) is a representation. The same formula defines a representation on any subspace of F(X)\mathcal F(X)F(X) stable under all the ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) (for infinite XXX one works with such subspaces — polynomials, smooth functions, square-integrable functions — as the context requires).

Proof. Linearity in ψ\psiψ is clear, and ρ(e)=id⁡\rho(e)=\idρ(e)=id. The dual representation [self-study]

For a general (not necessarily orthogonal) representation, the computation of the self-study paragraph above does not return the same matrices, and what it does return deserves its own name. Recall that the dual space V∗V^{*}V∗ of VVV is the space of linear maps φ ⁣:V→C\varphi\colon V\to\Cφ:V→C (functionals); given a basis (ei)(e_i)(ei​) of VVV, the dual basis (ei)(e^i)(ei) of V∗V^*V∗ is defined by ei(ej)=δije^i(e_j)=\delta_{ij}ei(ej​)=δij​. Linear functionals are functions on VVV, so the function-space construction applies to them, and it preserves linearity:

Proposition 3.10 (Dual representation). Let (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V) be a representation. Then (ρ∗(g)φ)(v):=φ(ρ(g−1)v),φ∈V∗, v∈V,\bigl(\rho^{*}(g)\varphi\bigr)(v):=\varphi\bigl(\rho(g^{-1})v\bigr), \qquad \varphi\in V^{*},\ v\in V,(ρ∗(g)φ)(v):=φ(ρ(g−1)v),φ∈V∗, v∈V, defines a representation (ρ∗,V∗)(\rho^{*},V^{*})(ρ∗,V∗), the dual (or contragredient) representation. In the dual basis its matrices are D∗(g)=D(g−1)T=(D(g)−1)T.D^{*}(g)=D(g^{-1})^{\mathsf T}=\bigl(D(g)^{-1}\bigr)^{\mathsf T}.D∗(g)=D(g−1)T=(D(g)−1)T.

Proof. Each ρ∗(g)\rho^{*}(g)ρ∗(g) maps functionals to functionals linearly, and the homomorphism property is Theorem 3.8 applied to the action of GGG on the set VVV via ρ\rhoρ. For the matrices: (ρ∗(g)ej)(ei)=ej(ρ(g−1)ei)=Dji(g−1)\bigl(\rho^{*}(g)e^j\bigr)(e_i)=e^j\bigl(\rho(g^{-1})e_i\bigr) =D_{ji}(g^{-1})(ρ∗(g)ej)(ei​)=ej(ρ(g−1)ei​)=Dji​(g−1), so expanding ρ∗(g)ej\rho^{*}(g)e^jρ∗(g)ej in the dual basis gives coefficients Dij∗(g)=Dji(g−1)D^{*}_{ij}(g)=D_{ji}(g^{-1})Dij∗​(g)=Dji​(g−1), which is the stated matrix. One can also verify directly that g↦D(g−1)Tg\mapsto D(g^{-1})^{\mathsf T}g↦D(g−1)T is a homomorphism: transposition and inversion each reverse the order of products, and two reversals cancel. 

If the matrices D(g)D(g)D(g) are unitary — and for a finite group Lecture 4 shows this can always be arranged — then D(g−1)=D(g)†D(g^{-1})=D(g)^{\dagger}D(g−1)=D(g)† and D∗(g)=D(g)‾D^{*}(g)=\overline{D(g)}D∗(g)=D(g)​: the dual representation is the complex conjugate representation. The physics reader has met this contragredient behaviour before: if kets transform as ∣ψ⟩↦ρ(g)∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}\mapsto\rho(g)\ket\psi∣ψ⟩↦ρ(g)∣ψ⟩, then bras transform with the conjugate matrices so that ⟨φ|ψ⟩\braket{\varphi}{\psi}⟨φ∣ψ⟩ stays invariant. The distinction between a representation and its conjugate, invisible when the matrices are real, prepares an important distinction in later SU(3)\SU(3)SU(3) study: the quark triplet 3\rep{3}3 and the antiquark antitriplet 3ˉ\bar{\rep{3}}3ˉ are conjugate representations. This course builds the dual/conjugate vocabulary but does not teach that particle-physics application; Jones, Ch. 8, §§8.3–8.4 is a further-reading destination.

The function-space construction is fully general, and we shall lean on it repeatedly. It is how a finite group acts on polynomials (the sign of a permutation, Lecture 1, and Example 3.9); how a point group acts on the displacement patterns of a vibrating molecule (Lecture 7); how a symmetry group acts on quantum wavefunctions, ψ(r⃗ )↦ψ(g−1r⃗ )\psi(\vec r\,)\mapsto\psi(g^{-1}\vec r\,)ψ(r)↦ψ(g−1r), which is the formula underlying degeneracies and selection rules (Lectures 6 and 8); and how the rotation group acts on functions on the sphere, whose stable subspaces are spanned by the spherical harmonics (Part II). The same formula serves in every one of these places.

Equivalence of representations

When are two representations “the same”? As with groups themselves (Lecture 1), the answer is: when a structure-preserving dictionary translates one into the other.

Definition 3.11 (Equivalence). Representations (ρV,V)(\rho_V,V)(ρV​,V) and (ρW,W)(\rho_W,W)(ρW​,W) of the same group GGG are equivalent, written ρV≅ρW\rho_V\cong\rho_WρV​≅ρW​, if there is an invertible linear map T ⁣:V→WT\colon V\to WT:V→W with T ρV(g)=ρW(g) Tfor all g∈G.T\,\rho_V(g)=\rho_W(g)\,T \quad\text{for all }g\in G .TρV​(g)=ρW​(g)Tfor all g∈G. Such a TTT is called an intertwiner (it lets the group action pass through it).

In matrix language: choosing bases and letting SSS be the matrix of TTT, equivalence reads DW(g)=S DV(g) S−1D_W(g)=S\,D_V(g)\,S^{-1}DW​(g)=SDV​(g)S−1 for all ggg, with one and the same SSS. In particular the matrices of a single representation in two different bases are related this way — equivalent representations are one representation written in different coordinates, and all notions we define (irreducibility, characters, multiplicities) will be invariant under equivalence.

Two representations can be combined into one bigger representation.

Definition 3.12 (Direct sum). Let (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V) and (σ,W)(\sigma,W)(σ,W) be representations of GGG. The space V⊕WV\oplus WV⊕W consists of all pairs (v,w)(v,w)(v,w) with v∈Vv\in Vv∈V and w∈Ww\in Ww∈W. The direct sum ρ⊕σ\rho\oplus\sigmaρ⊕σ of the two representations acts on it by (ρ⊕σ)(g) (v,w)=(ρ(g)v,  σ(g)w).(\rho\oplus\sigma)(g)\,(v,w)=\bigl(\rho(g)v,\;\sigma(g)w\bigr).(ρ⊕σ)(g)(v,w)=(ρ(g)v,σ(g)w).

Each component of the pair is transformed on its own, so ρ⊕σ\rho\oplus\sigmaρ⊕σ is again a representation. In a basis of VVV followed by a basis of WWW, its matrices are block-diagonal: DV⊕W(g)=(DV(g)00DW(g)).D_{V\oplus W}(g)= \begin{pmatrix} D_V(g) & 0\\ 0 & D_W(g) \end{pmatrix}.DV⊕W​(g)=(DV​(g)0​0DW​(g)​).

Example 3.13 (A representation that splits over C\CC but not over R\RR). Let C3=⟨r⟩C_3=\langle r\rangleC3​=⟨r⟩ act on C2\C^2C2 through the rotation matrices of Example 3.4: D(rk)=R(2πk/3)D(r^k)=R(2\pi k/3)D(rk)=R(2πk/3). The vectors v±=(1∓i)v_\pm=\binom{1}{\mp\ii}v±​=(∓i1​) are eigenvectors of every rotation: R(θ)(1∓i)=(cos⁡θ±isin⁡θsin⁡θ∓icos⁡θ)=e±iθ(1∓i),R(\theta)\binom{1}{\mp\ii} =\binom{\cos\theta\pm\ii\sin\theta}{\sin\theta\mp\ii\cos\theta} =e^{\pm\ii\theta}\binom{1}{\mp\ii},R(θ)(∓i1​)=(sinθ∓icosθcosθ±isinθ​)=e±iθ(∓i1​), as multiplying out confirms. So in the basis (v+,v−)(v_+,v_-)(v+​,v−​) the matrices become S−1D(rk) S=(ωk00ω−k),ω=e2πi/3,S^{-1}D(r^k)\,S= \begin{pmatrix} \omega^{k}&0\\ 0&\omega^{-k} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \omega=e^{2\pi\ii/3},S−1D(rk)S=(ωk0​0ω−k​),ω=e2πi/3, where S=(v+ ∣ v−)S=(v_+\,|\,v_-)S=(v+​∣v−​) is the matrix whose columns are the vectors v+v_+v+​ and v−v_-v−​. So the rotation representation of C3C_3C3​ is equivalent, over C\CC, to the direct sum χ1⊕χ2\chi_1\oplus\chi_{2}χ1​⊕χ2​ (indices mod 333, so χ2=χ−1\chi_2=\chi_{-1}χ2​=χ−1​) of two of the one-dimensional representations of Example 3.3. The diagonal matrix above has the 1×11\times11×1 blocks χ1(rk)=ωk\chi_1(r^k)=\omega^kχ1​(rk)=ωk and χ2(rk)=ω2k=ω−k\chi_2(r^k)=\omega^{2k}=\omega^{-k}χ2​(rk)=ω2k=ω−k. Notice that the diagonalising basis is genuinely complex: over the reals a rotation through 2π/32\pi/32π/3 has no eigenvector at all, and the representation cannot be split. Working over C\CC is not a convenience but a structural choice, and it is the right one for quantum mechanics, where state spaces are complex from the outset.

The equivalence just found does two things at once: it relates two ways of writing the same representation, and it splits the rotation representation into two smaller ones. Splitting is the theme of the next lecture. It asks when a representation contains a smaller one, whether the smaller one always comes with a complement, and what the pieces that cannot be split further look like.

Summary

  • Representation. A homomorphism G→GL(V)G\to\GL(V)G→GL(V), equivalently a linear action of GGG on VVV (Definition 3.1); in a basis, a matrix representation D(gh)=D(g)D(h)D(gh)=D(g)D(h)D(gh)=D(g)D(h). First examples: the one-dimensional χk\chi_kχk​ of CnC_nCn​, the defining representation of DnD_nDn​, permutation representations.
  • Function space. GGG acts on functions by (ρ(g)ψ)(x)=ψ(g−1x)(\rho(g)\psi)(x)=\psi(g^{-1}x)(ρ(g)ψ)(x)=ψ(g−1x) (Theorem 3.8); the inverse keeps the order of products. The self-study section carries the dual representation.
  • Equivalence. Two representations are the same when an invertible intertwiner TTT with TρV(g)=ρW(g)TT\rho_V(g)=\rho_W(g)TTρV​(g)=ρW​(g)T connects them (Definition 3.11). Over C\CC the rotation representation of C3C_3C3​ is equivalent to the direct sum χ1⊕χ2\chi_1\oplus\chi_2χ1​⊕χ2​ (Definition 3.12); over R\RR it cannot be split.
