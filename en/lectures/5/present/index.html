Lecture 5

Characters

Lecture 4 decomposed a representation by hunting for invariant subspaces. This lecture replaces the hunt by arithmetic. The character of a representation is the trace of its matrices, one number per group element. Averaging over the group, the argument of Lecture 4, shows that the characters of the irreducible representations are orthonormal. From this follows everything else: characters count multiplicities, test irreducibility, determine a representation, and fix the dimensions of the irreducible representations. We build the character table of C3v≅S3C_{3v}\cong S_3C3v​≅S3​, the symmetry group of the ammonia molecule; the tutorial builds that of C3C_3C3​.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Compute characters, and use them to find multiplicities and test irreducibility.
  • Count irreducible representations and their dimensions.
  • Build a small character table.

Characters and class functions

Can the irreducible content of a representation be read off without finding a single invariant subspace? It can, from traces.

Definition 5.1 (Character). The character of a representation (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V) of GGG is the function χρ ⁣:G→C,χρ(g)=tr⁡ρ(g)=tr⁡D(g).\chi_\rho\colon G\to\C, \qquad \chi_\rho(g)=\tr\rho(g)=\tr D(g).χρ​:G→C,χρ​(g)=trρ(g)=trD(g). It is well defined, since the trace is the same in every basis. The characters of the irreducible representations ρ(i)\rho^{(i)}ρ(i) are written χi\chi_iχi​ and called irreducible characters. Note that χρ(e)=tr⁡ id⁡V=dim⁡V\chi_\rho(e)=\tr\,\id_V=\dim Vχρ​(e)=tridV​=dimV, so the character remembers the dimension.

Proposition 5.2 (Basic properties). Let χ\chiχ be the character of (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V), with GGG finite. Then:

  1. equivalent representations have equal characters;

  2. χ(hgh−1)=χ(g)\chi(hgh^{-1})=\chi(g)χ(hgh−1)=χ(g) for all g,h∈Gg,h\in Gg,h∈G: characters are constant on conjugacy classes;

  3. χ(g−1)=χ(g)‾\chi(g^{-1})=\overline{\chi(g)}χ(g−1)=χ(g)​;

  4. characters add on direct sums: χρ⊕σ=χρ+χσ\chi_{\rho\oplus\sigma}=\chi_\rho+\chi_\sigmaχρ⊕σ​=χρ​+χσ​.

Proof. (i) and (ii) are the invariance of the trace under conjugation: tr⁡(SD(g)S−1)=tr⁡D(g)andtr⁡(D(h)D(g)D(h)−1)=tr⁡D(g).\tr(SD(g)S^{-1})=\tr D(g)\quad\text{and}\quad \tr\bigl(D(h)D(g)D(h)^{-1}\bigr)=\tr D(g).tr(SD(g)S−1)=trD(g)andtr(D(h)D(g)D(h)−1)=trD(g). (iii) By Lecture 4 we may take D(g)D(g)D(g) unitary, so D(g−1)=D(g)−1=D(g)†,D(g^{-1})=D(g)^{-1}=D(g)^{\dagger},D(g−1)=D(g)−1=D(g)†, whose trace is the complex conjugate of tr⁡D(g)\tr D(g)trD(g).

(iv) In a basis adapted to the direct sum the matrices are block-diagonal, and the trace is the sum of the traces of the blocks. 

Because characters are constant on conjugacy classes, they lie in a special finite-dimensional space.

Definition 5.3 (Class functions). A class function on GGG is a function G→CG\to\CG→C that is constant on each conjugacy class. Class functions form a vector space CF(G)\mathrm{CF}(G)CF(G) whose dimension is the number of conjugacy classes, since each class carries one free value. We give it the inner product ⟨φ,ψ⟩G=1∣G∣∑g∈Gφ(g)‾ ψ(g)=1∣G∣∑classes C∣C∣  φ(gC)‾ ψ(gC).\inner{\varphi}{\psi}_G =\frac{1}{\abs G}\sum_{g\in G}\overline{\varphi(g)}\,\psi(g) =\frac{1}{\abs G}\sum_{\text{classes }C}\abs{C}\; \overline{\varphi(g_C)}\,\psi(g_C).⟨φ,ψ⟩G​=∣G∣1​g∈G∑​φ(g)​ψ(g)=∣G∣1​classes C∑​∣C∣φ(gC​)​ψ(gC​). In practice one computes with the second form, where gCg_CgC​ is any representative of CCC.

One more property takes one line to prove. Lecture 7 uses it to count the vibrations of a molecule.

Lemma 5.4 (Fixed-point count). The character of a permutation representation (Lecture 3) counts fixed points: χperm(g)=#{ x∈X:g⋅x=x }.\chi_{\mathrm{perm}}(g)=\#\{\,x\in X : g\cdot x=x\,\}.χperm​(g)=#{x∈X:g⋅x=x}.

Proof. The diagonal entries of the permutation matrix are Dxx(g)=δx, g⋅xD_{xx}(g)=\delta_{x,\,g\cdot x}Dxx​(g)=δx,g⋅x​. So each fixed point contributes a 111. 

For S3S_3S3​ permuting {1,2,3}\{1,2,3\}{1,2,3}, for instance, the identity fixes all three points, a transposition one and a 333-cycle none: χperm=(3,1,0)\chi_{\mathrm{perm}}=(3,1,0)χperm​=(3,1,0) on the three classes.

The averaging lemma, and orthogonality

Lecture 4 averaged an inner product over the group. Now we average the operators themselves.

On C3\C^3C3 with S3S_3S3​ permuting the coordinates, for instance, the only invariant vectors are the multiples of (1,1,1)(1,1,1)(1,1,1), and indeed 16(3+3⋅1+2⋅0)=1\frac16(3+3\cdot1+2\cdot0)=161​(3+3⋅1+2⋅0)=1.

The lemma counts invariant vectors. To count intertwiners, apply it to the right space. Let VVV and WWW be representations, and let Hom⁡(V,W)\Hom(V,W)Hom(V,W) be the space of all linear maps T ⁣:V→WT\colon V\to WT:V→W, that is, of all dim⁡W×dim⁡V\dim W\times\dim VdimW×dimV matrices. Lemma 5.6. The character of Hom⁡(V,W)\Hom(V,W)Hom(V,W) is g↦χV(g)‾ χW(g)g\mapsto\overline{\chi_V(g)}\,\chi_W(g)g↦χV​(g)​χW​(g).

Proof. Fix ggg. It acts on matrices TTT by T↦A TBT\mapsto A\,TBT↦ATB, where A=DW(g)A=D_W(g)A=DW​(g) and B=DV(g−1)B=D_V(g^{-1})B=DV​(g−1). Theorem 5.7 (Characters count intertwiners). For representations VVV and WWW of a finite group, ⟨χV,χW⟩G=dim⁡Hom⁡G(V,W).\inner{\chi_V}{\chi_W}_G=\dim\Hom_G(V,W).⟨χV​,χW​⟩G​=dimHomG​(V,W). The irreducible characters are orthonormal vectors in CF(G)\mathrm{CF}(G)CF(G), whose dimension is the number of classes. So a finite group has at most as many irreducible representations as conjugacy classes. In other words, every finite group has finitely many irreducible representations.

Characters determine the representation

Decompose a representation by Maschke’s theorem, V≅⨁jmj V(j)V\cong\bigoplus_j m_j\,V^{(j)}V≅⨁j​mj​V(j). Characters add over direct sums, so χV=∑jmj χj\chi_V=\sum_j m_j\,\chi_jχV​=∑j​mj​χj​, and orthonormality picks out each coefficient.

Theorem 5.8 (Multiplicity formula). Let (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V) be any representation, with character χV\chi_VχV​ and decomposition V≅⨁jmj V(j)V\cong\bigoplus_j m_j\,V^{(j)}V≅⨁j​mj​V(j). Then mi=⟨χi,χV⟩G.m_i=\inner{\chi_i}{\chi_V}_G .mi​=⟨χi​,χV​⟩G​. So the multiplicities are inner products, computable from the character alone, with no basis-hunting. Moreover, they do not depend on how VVV was decomposed.

Proof. ⟨χi,χV⟩G=∑jmj⟨χi,χj⟩G=mi\inner{\chi_i}{\chi_V}_G=\sum_jm_j\inner{\chi_i}{\chi_j}_G=m_i⟨χi​,χV​⟩G​=∑j​mj​⟨χi​,χj​⟩G​=mi​.

Proof. Equivalent representations have equal characters (Proposition 5.2(i)).

Theorems that once needed structural arguments have become arithmetic. The bound of Section 5.2 is in fact an equality:

Theorem 5.12. The number of inequivalent complex irreducible representations of a finite group equals the number of its conjugacy classes.

We showed above that there are at most as many irreducible representations as classes. It remains to show that there are no fewer, which the self-study section does. What matters most to us is that the irreducible characters form an orthonormal basis of the class functions.

Character tables

All the character theory of a finite group fits in a single square array, the character table. It has one row per irreducible character and one column per conjugacy class. The first column is the class of the identity, so it lists the dimensions nin_ini​; the first row is the trivial character, all 111s; the class sizes are recorded above the columns, since the inner product needs them. The rows are orthonormal with weights ∣C∣/∣G∣\abs C/\abs G∣C∣/∣G∣.

To build a table: find the classes (Lecture 2); solve ∑ini2=∣G∣\sum_in_i^2=\abs G∑i​ni2​=∣G∣ with as many nin_ini​ as classes; write down the one-dimensional characters, which are homomorphisms G→C∖{0}G\to\C\setminus\{0\}G→C∖{0}; fill in the rest from traces of known matrices or from ∑ini χi(g)=0\sum_i n_i\,\chi_i(g)=0∑i​ni​χi​(g)=0; check orthonormality. For an abelian group such as C3C_3C3​ every class is a single element and every row is a χk\chi_kχk​ (Lecture 4); the tutorial writes out that table.
