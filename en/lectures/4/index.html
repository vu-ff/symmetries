Lecture 4

Schur’s lemma, orthogonality, and the group algebra

What this lecture does

A single, almost trivial-looking observation about intertwiners — Schur’s lemma — turns out to control the entire structure of finite group representations. From it we derive the orthogonality of matrix elements, and, via the regular representation and the group algebra, the two great counting theorems: the squared dimensions of the irreducible representations sum to the group order, and the number of irreducible representations equals the number of conjugacy classes. By the end of the lecture, the representation theory of a finite group is reduced to a finite, sharply constrained bookkeeping problem.

Schur’s lemma

Recall from Lecture 3 that an intertwiner between representations (ρV,V)(\rho_V,V)(ρV​,V) and (ρW,W)(\rho_W,W)(ρW​,W) of GGG is a linear map T ⁣:V→WT\colon V\to WT:V→W with T ρV(g)=ρW(g) Tfor all g∈G;T\,\rho_V(g)=\rho_W(g)\,T \qquad\text{for all }g\in G ;TρV​(g)=ρW​(g)Tfor all g∈G; there we required TTT invertible and called the representations equivalent. Now we drop invertibility and study the vector space of all intertwiners, denoted Hom⁡G(V,W)\Hom_G(V,W)HomG​(V,W). The decisive observation is one line long:

Lemma 4.1. If T∈Hom⁡G(V,W)T\in\Hom_G(V,W)T∈HomG​(V,W), then ker⁡T\ker TkerT is an invariant subspace of VVV and im⁡T\im TimT is an invariant subspace of WWW.

Proof. If Tv=0Tv=0Tv=0 then TρV(g)v=ρW(g)Tv=0T\rho_V(g)v=\rho_W(g)Tv=0TρV​(g)v=ρW​(g)Tv=0, so ρV(g)v∈ker⁡T\rho_V(g)v\in\ker TρV​(g)v∈kerT. And ρW(g) Tv=TρV(g)v∈im⁡T\rho_W(g)\,Tv=T\rho_V(g)v\in\im TρW​(g)Tv=TρV​(g)v∈imT. 

For irreducible representations, invariant subspaces are scarce, and the lemma snaps shut into a powerful dichotomy.

Theorem 4.2 (Schur’s lemma, first form). Let VVV and WWW be irreducible representations of GGG and T∈Hom⁡G(V,W)T\in\Hom_G(V,W)T∈HomG​(V,W). Then either T=0T=0T=0 or TTT is an isomorphism (so V≅WV\cong WV≅W). In particular, between inequivalent irreducible representations the only intertwiner is zero.

Proof. Suppose T≠0T\neq0T=0. By Lemma 4.1, ker⁡T\ker TkerT is an invariant subspace of the irreducible VVV, and ker⁡T≠V\ker T\neq VkerT=V since T≠0T\neq0T=0; hence ker⁡T={0}\ker T=\{0\}kerT={0} and TTT is injective. Likewise im⁡T\im TimT is a nonzero invariant subspace of the irreducible WWW, hence im⁡T=W\im T=WimT=W: TTT is surjective. 

Theorem 4.3 (Schur’s lemma, second form). Let (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V) be a finite-dimensional complex irreducible representation, and let T∈Hom⁡G(V,V)T\in\Hom_G(V,V)T∈HomG​(V,V), i.e. Tρ(g)=ρ(g)TT\rho(g)=\rho(g)TTρ(g)=ρ(g)T for all ggg. Corollary 4.5 (Abelian groups). Every complex irreducible representation of an abelian group is one-dimensional.

Proof. Let (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V) be irreducible and GGG abelian. For fixed h∈Gh\in Gh∈G the operator ρ(h)\rho(h)ρ(h) commutes with every ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) — that is, ρ(h)∈Hom⁡G(V,V)\rho(h)\in\Hom_G(V,V)ρ(h)∈HomG​(V,V) — so ρ(h)=λh id⁡V\rho(h)=\lambda_h\,\id_Vρ(h)=λh​idV​ by the second form. This explains, from first principles, what Lecture 3 found by hand: the rotation representation of C3C_3C3​ had to split into one-dimensional pieces over C\CC. Remark 4.6 (Complex is essential). The second form fails over R\RR, where eigenvalues need not exist. Let C4=⟨r⟩C_4=\langle r\rangleC4​=⟨r⟩ act irreducibly on R2\R^2R2 by the quarter-turn J=R(π/2)=(0−110)J=R(\pi/2)=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)J=R(π/2)=(01​−10​). Throughout this course “irreducible” means irreducible over C\CC unless flagged otherwise.

Remark 4.7 (The physics of Schur’s lemma). Read physically, the second form says: an operator that commutes with an irreducible symmetry action is a multiple of the identity. If the Hamiltonian of a system commutes with all the ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) — the defining property of a symmetry, as Lecture 7 will set up — then on an irreducible representation occurring with multiplicity one the Hamiltonian is forced to be a scalar: one energy for the whole representation. More generally, on the sum of all mim_imi​ copies of V(i)V^{(i)}V(i) — which Lecture 5 will name the isotypic component and write V(i)⊗CmiV^{(i)}\otimes\C^{m_i}V(i)⊗Cmi​ — the symmetry acts as ρ(i)(g)⊗1\rho^{(i)}(g)\otimes\mathbb 1ρ(i)(g)⊗1, and its commuting operators have the form 1⊗Fi\mathbb 1\otimes F_i1⊗Fi​. They may therefore mix the mim_imi​ equivalent copies; after diagonalising FiF_iFi​, each of its eigenvalues still has the dim⁡V(i)\dim V^{(i)}dimV(i)-fold multiplet forced by symmetry. More precisely, if an eigenvalue of FiF_iFi​ has multiplicity qqq, its total degeneracy on the isotypic component is qdim⁡V(i)q\dim V^{(i)}qdimV(i); only the factor dim⁡V(i)\dim V^{(i)}dimV(i) is forced by this complex irreducible type. Irreducible representations are thus the natural candidates for the degenerate multiplets of quantum mechanics, and Schur’s lemma is the reason. The same mechanism, applied to operators that intertwine different irreducible blocks, will give the Wigner–Eckart theorem and the selection rules of Lecture 7.

Orthogonality of matrix elements

Schur’s lemma is a statement about operators. Combined with the averaging trick of Lecture 3, it becomes a statement about numbers — the matrix elements of the irreducible representations — and these numbers turn out to be orthogonal in a precise sense.

Fix once and for all a complete list ρ(1),ρ(2),…\rho^{(1)},\rho^{(2)},\dotsρ(1),ρ(2),… of pairwise inequivalent irreducible representations of the finite group GGG, on spaces V(i)V^{(i)}V(i) of dimensions nin_ini​, and choose bases making the matrices D(i)(g)D^{(i)}(g)D(i)(g) unitary (always possible, Lecture 3). The completeness of the list — that there are finitely many and that we have them all — is established in the next section; nothing in the present one depends on it.

We regard each matrix element as a complex-valued function on the group, g↦Dμν(i)(g)g\mapsto D^{(i)}_{\mu\nu}(g)g↦Dμν(i)​(g), and we equip the ∣G∣\abs G∣G∣-dimensional space of all functions ψ ⁣:G→C\psi\colon G\to\Cψ:G→C with the inner product ⟨φ,ψ⟩G=1∣G∣∑g∈Gφ(g)‾ ψ(g),\inner{\varphi}{\psi}_G =\frac{1}{\abs G}\sum_{g\in G}\overline{\varphi(g)}\,\psi(g),⟨φ,ψ⟩G​=∣G∣1​g∈G∑​φ(g)​ψ(g), conjugate-linear in the first argument as always.

The group algebra and the regular representation

To convert the inequality of Corollary 4.10 into an equality, we deploy one special representation that contains everything: the group acting on itself.

Definition 4.12 (Group algebra). The group algebra C[G]\C[G]C[G] is the ∣G∣\abs G∣G∣-dimensional vector space with one basis vector ege_geg​ for each g∈Gg\in Gg∈G, equipped with the bilinear multiplication extending the group law: (∑gag eg)(∑hbh eh)=∑g,hagbh egh.\Bigl(\sum_g a_g\,e_g\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_h b_h\,e_h\Bigr) =\sum_{g,h}a_gb_h\,e_{gh}.(g∑​ag​eg​)(h∑​bh​eh​)=g,h∑​ag​bh​egh​. It is an associative algebra (a vector space with a bilinear associative product) with unit eee_eee​, in which GGG sits as the basis. The regular representation of GGG is left multiplication on this space: ρreg(g) eh=egh,\rho^{\mathrm{reg}}(g)\,e_h=e_{gh},ρreg(g)eh​=egh​, the permutation representation (Lecture 3) of the action of GGG on itself by left translation; its dimension is ∣G∣\abs G∣G∣.

Every representation ρ\rhoρ of the group extends to the algebra by linearity, ρ(∑ageg):=∑agρ(g)\rho(\sum a_ge_g):=\sum a_g\rho(g)ρ(∑ag​eg​):=∑ag​ρ(g), a fact we will use silently below and seriously in the starred subsection.

Two short lemmas unlock the structure of the regular representation. The first says that an intertwiner out of C[G]\C[G]C[G] is freely specified by one vector; the second says that intertwiner dimensions count multiplicities.

Counting the irreducibles: conjugacy classes

How many irreducible representations does a finite group have? The answer ties the representation theory back to the conjugacy structure of Lecture 2, and is the second pillar (with ∑ni2=∣G∣\sum n_i^2=\abs G∑ni2​=∣G∣) on which the character tables of Lecture 5 stand.

Theorem 4.17. The number of inequivalent complex irreducible representations of a finite group equals the number of its conjugacy classes.

The proof identifies both numbers with the dimension of one and the same space: the centre of the group algebra, Z(C[G])={ z∈C[G]  :  za=az for all a∈C[G] }.Z\bigl(\C[G]\bigr) =\bigl\{\,z\in\C[G]\;:\;za=az\ \text{for all }a\in\C[G]\,\bigr\}.Z(C[G])={z∈C[G]:za=az for all a∈C[G]}. That the class count appears there is elementary and we prove it at once; that the representation count does is the starred material below.

Lemma 4.18 (Class sums). For each conjugacy class C⊆GC\subseteq GC⊆G let zC=∑g∈Ceg∈C[G]z_C=\sum_{g\in C}e_g\in\C[G]zC​=∑g∈C​eg​∈C[G]. The class sums form a basis of Z(C[G])Z(\C[G])Z(C[G]); in particular dim⁡Z(C[G])=#{conjugacy classes of G}.\dim Z\bigl(\C[G]\bigr)=\#\{\text{conjugacy classes of }G\}.dimZ(C[G])=#{conjugacy classes of G}.

Proof. An element a=∑gagega=\sum_g a_ge_ga=∑g​ag​eg​ is central iff it commutes with every basis element, i.e. eh a eh−1=ae_h\,a\,e_{h}^{-1}=aeh​aeh−1​=a for all hhh. The left side is ∑gagehgh−1=∑gah−1gh eg\sum_g a_g e_{hgh^{-1}}=\sum_g a_{h^{-1}gh}\,e_g∑g​ag​ehgh−1​=∑g​ah−1gh​eg​, so centrality says exactly that the coefficient function is constant on conjugacy classes: ahgh−1=aga_{hgh^{-1}}=a_gahgh−1​=ag​ for all g,hg,hg,h. Such elements are precisely the linear combinations of class sums, and the zCz_CzC​ are linearly independent because distinct classes are disjoint. 

Structure of the remaining argument. Extending each irreducible representation linearly to the algebra gives a map Φ ⁣:  C[G]⟶⨁iEnd⁡(V(i)),Φ(a)=(ρ(1)(a), ρ(2)(a),… ),\Phi\colon\;\C[G]\longrightarrow \bigoplus_i\End\bigl(V^{(i)}\bigr), \qquad \Phi(a)=\bigl(\rho^{(1)}(a),\,\rho^{(2)}(a),\dots\bigr),Φ:C[G]⟶i⨁​End(V(i)),Φ(a)=(ρ(1)(a),ρ(2)(a),…), into the direct sum of full matrix algebras. One shows Φ\PhiΦ is an isomorphism of algebras — injective because the regular representation is faithful and built from the V(i)V^{(i)}V(i), surjective by the dimension count ∑ni2=∣G∣\sum n_i^2=\abs G∑ni2​=∣G∣ of Theorem 4.15. The centre of a direct sum of matrix algebras is easy to compute: each block contributes only scalar matrices (Schur again, in algebraic clothing), one independent scalar per block. Hence dim⁡Z(C[G])\dim Z(\C[G])dimZ(C[G]) equals the number of blocks, i.e. of irreducible representations, and Lemma 4.18 finishes the proof. The details follow, starred; the statement of Theorem 4.17 is what the rest of the course uses.

⋆\star⋆ The group algebra is a sum of matrix algebras

Theorem 4.19 (⋆\star⋆). The map Φ\PhiΦ above is an isomorphism of algebras: C[G]  ≅  ⨁iEnd⁡(V(i))  ≅  ⨁iMni(C).\C[G]\;\cong\;\bigoplus_i\End\bigl(V^{(i)}\bigr) \;\cong\;\bigoplus_i M_{n_i}(\C).C[G]≅i⨁​End(V(i))≅i⨁​Mni​​(C).

Proof (⋆\star⋆). Φ\PhiΦ is an algebra homomorphism since each extended ρ(i)\rho^{(i)}ρ(i) is (linearity, plus multiplicativity on the basis).

Injectivity. Suppose Φ(a)=0\Phi(a)=0Φ(a)=0, i.e. aaa acts as zero in every irreducible representation. By Maschke’s theorem every representation is a direct sum of irreducibles, so aaa acts as zero in every representation of GGG — in particular in the regular representation. But the regular representation detects every algebra element: ρreg(a) ee=a\rho^{\mathrm{reg}}(a)\,e_e=aρreg(a)ee​=a. Hence a=0a=0a=0.

Surjectivity. Both sides have the same finite dimension, dim⁡C[G]=∣G∣=∑ini2=∑idim⁡End⁡(V(i))\dim\C[G]=\abs G=\sum_in_i^2=\sum_i\dim\End(V^{(i)})dimC[G]=∣G∣=∑i​ni2​=∑i​dimEnd(V(i)) by Theorem 4.15; an injective linear map between spaces of equal finite dimension is surjective. 

Lemma 4.20 (⋆\star⋆). The centre of the matrix algebra End⁡(V)≅Mn(C)\End(V)\cong M_n(\C)End(V)≅Mn​(C) consists of the scalar matrices: Z(Mn(C))=C 1Z(M_n(\C))=\C\,\mathbb 1Z(Mn​(C))=C1.

Proof (⋆\star⋆). Let ZZZ commute with all matrices, in particular with the matrix units EμνE^{\mu\nu}Eμν. Comparing entries of ZEμν=EμνZZE^{\mu\nu}=E^{\mu\nu}ZZEμν=EμνZ gives Zαμδνβ=δαμZνβZ_{\alpha\mu}\delta_{\nu\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\mu}Z_{\nu\beta}Zαμ​δνβ​=δαμ​Zνβ​ for all indices; setting α=μ\alpha=\muα=μ, β=ν\beta=\nuβ=ν yields Zμμ=ZννZ_{\mu\mu}=Z_{\nu\nu}Zμμ​=Zνν​, and setting β=ν\beta=\nuβ=ν, α≠μ\alpha\neq\muα=μ yields Zαμ=0Z_{\alpha\mu}=0Zαμ​=0 off the diagonal. 

Proof of Theorem 4.17 (⋆\star⋆). An element of the centre of a direct sum of algebras is a tuple of central elements of the summands, so by Theorem 4.19 and Lemma 4.20, Z(C[G])  ≅  ⨁iZ(End⁡(V(i)))=⨁iC 1V(i),Z\bigl(\C[G]\bigr)\;\cong\;\bigoplus_i Z\bigl(\End(V^{(i)})\bigr) =\bigoplus_i\C\,\mathbb 1_{V^{(i)}} ,Z(C[G])≅i⨁​Z(End(V(i)))=i⨁​C1V(i)​, of dimension equal to the number of irreducible representations. By Lemma 4.18 the same dimension is the number of conjugacy classes. 

Theorem 4.19 deserves a comment beyond its role here: it says the group algebra of a finite group is, as an algebra, nothing but a direct sum of full matrix algebras, with block sizes the dimensions of the irreducible representations. The great orthogonality theorem is this statement seen through the inner product; the character theory of Lecture 5 is this statement seen through the trace.

Corollary 4.22. A finite group is abelian if and only if all of its irreducible representations are one-dimensional, and in that case it has exactly ∣G∣\abs G∣G∣ of them.

Proof. If GGG is abelian, Corollary 4.5 gives ni=1n_i=1ni​=1 for all iii, and then ∑ini2=∣G∣\sum_in_i^2=\abs G∑i​ni2​=∣G∣ forces ∣G∣\abs G∣G∣ of them. Fourier analysis on a finite group

The two counting theorems upgrade the orthogonality of matrix elements into a completeness statement.

Theorem 4.23 (Completeness of matrix elements). The functions ni Dμν(i)\sqrt{n_i}\,D^{(i)}_{\mu\nu}ni​​Dμν(i)​ (iii over the irreducible representations, 1≤μ,ν≤ni1\leq\mu,\nu\leq n_i1≤μ,ν≤ni​, matrices unitary) form an orthonormal basis of the space of all functions G→CG\to\CG→C with the inner product ⟨⋅,⋅⟩G\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}_G⟨⋅,⋅⟩G​. Proof. Orthonormality is Theorem 4.9; the count ∑ini2=∣G∣\sum_in_i^2=\abs G∑i​ni2​=∣G∣ (Theorem 4.15) says the set has exactly the dimension of the function space, so it is a basis. The coefficient formula is the standard orthonormal expansion. 

For G=CnG=C_nG=Cn​ the matrix elements are the one-dimensional representations χk(rm)=e2πikm/n\chi_k(r^m)=e^{2\pi\ii km/n}χk​(rm)=e2πikm/n (Lecture 5 will call them characters), and the theorem is the discrete Fourier transform: expansion of an arbitrary function of mmm in exponential modes, with the familiar coefficient integrals replaced by averages over the group. Everything in this lecture is, from this angle, the generalisation of Fourier analysis to noncommutative groups — the “frequencies” becoming matrix-valued. The same idea, pushed from finite groups to compact Lie groups in Part II, will exhibit the spherical harmonics as matrix elements of the rotation group, with the Peter–Weyl theorem in the role of Theorem 4.23.

Summary

  • Schur’s lemma. An intertwiner of irreducibles is zero or an isomorphism (Theorem 4.2); over C\CC it is a scalar (Theorem 4.3), so abelian groups have one-dimensional irreducibles (Corollary 4.5).
  • Great orthogonality theorem. Matrix elements of the irreducibles are mutually orthogonal, with norm fixed by the dimension (Theorem 4.9).
  • Sum of squares. ∑ini2=∣G∣\sum_i n_i^2=\lvert G\rvert∑i​ni2​=∣G∣, from decomposing the regular representation (Theorem 4.15).
  • Counting. The number of irreducibles equals the number of conjugacy classes (Theorem 4.17).
  • Completeness. The matrix elements of the irreducibles form a basis of the functions on GGG (Theorem 4.23).

Self-check

5 questions on the ideas this lecture is most often misread on. Answer before you look — a wrong answer you thought about is worth more than a right one you guessed.

1Over C, an endomorphism intertwining an irreducible representation with itself must be what?

Use the second form of Schur's lemma.

Schur's lemma makes the commutant of a complex irreducible representation scalar.

2What identity must the irreducible dimensions dᵢ satisfy for a finite group?

Decompose the regular representation.

Each irrep of dimension dᵢ occurs dᵢ times, giving Σdᵢ²=|G|.

3For C₃, what is the sum of the squared dimensions of the irreducible representations?

C₃ is abelian, so ask first how large each irreducible can be.

Three one-dimensional irreducibles give 1²+1²+1²=3, which is |C₃|.

4Which dimensions form the complete list of irreducibles of S₃?

Their squares must sum to six.

1²+1²+2²=6, and the three types match the three conjugacy classes.

5If the squared dimensions of the irreducibles you have found sum to less than |G|, what follows?

The regular representation accounts for all |G| dimensions.

A deficit means at least one irreducible type is still missing.
