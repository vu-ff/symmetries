Lecture 4

Irreducibility, averaging, and Schur’s lemma

Lecture 3 ended with a representation that split into two smaller ones. This lecture asks when that happens and what the pieces are. A representation is irreducible when it has no smaller representation inside it. Averaging an inner product over the group makes every representation of a finite group unitary, and from unitarity follows Maschke’s theorem: every representation is a direct sum of irreducible ones. Schur’s lemma then says how rigid the irreducible pieces are. Only multiples of the identity commute with an irreducible representation, and that is the reason symmetric Hamiltonians have degenerate levels.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Find invariant subspaces and test irreducibility.
  • Make a representation unitary by averaging, and split it by Maschke’s theorem.
  • Apply Schur’s lemma.

Invariant subspaces and irreducibility

The last example of Lecture 3 displayed a representation containing smaller ones: the rotations of C3C_3C3​ preserve the lines Cv+\C v_+Cv+​ and Cv−\C v_-Cv−​. The general notions:

Definition 4.1 (Invariant subspace; irreducibility). Let (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V) be a representation. A subspace W⊆VW\subseteq VW⊆V is invariant if ρ(g)W⊆Wfor all g∈G.\rho(g)W\subseteq W \quad\text{for all } g\in G.ρ(g)W⊆Wfor all g∈G. Equivalently, ρ(g)W=W\rho(g)W=Wρ(g)W=W (it suffices to act on the condition above with ρ(g−1)\rho(g^{-1})ρ(g−1)). An invariant subspace carries the subrepresentation ρ∣W ⁣:g↦ρ(g)∣W.\rho|_W\colon g\mapsto\rho(g)|_W.ρ∣W​:g↦ρ(g)∣W​. The representation is irreducible if dim⁡V≥1\dim V\geq 1dimV≥1 and its only invariant subspaces are {0}\{0\}{0} and VVV; otherwise it is reducible. Every one-dimensional representation is irreducible.

A line Cv\C vCv is invariant exactly when vvv is an eigenvector of every ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) at once. So a two-dimensional representation is reducible exactly when its operators have a common eigenvector.

Example 4.2 (Inside the permutation representation). In the permutation representation of SnS_nSn​ on Cn\C^nCn (Lecture 3), the line L=C u0,u0=e1+e2+⋯+en,L=\C\,u_0, \qquad u_0=e_1+e_2+\dots+e_n,L=Cu0​,u0​=e1​+e2​+⋯+en​, is invariant: permuting the summands leaves u0u_0u0​ fixed, so LLL carries a copy of the trivial representation. A second invariant subspace is V0={ v∈Cn:∑ivi=0 },V_0=\Bigl\{\,v\in\C^n : \textstyle\sum_i v_i=0\,\Bigr\},V0​={v∈Cn:∑i​vi​=0}, of dimension n−1n-1n−1: permuting the components of vvv does not change their sum. So the permutation representation is reducible for every n≥2n\geq 2n≥2, and Cn=L⊕V0\C^n=L\oplus V_0Cn=L⊕V0​ as vector spaces, both summands invariant. The subrepresentation on V0V_0V0​ is called the standard representation of SnS_nSn​; for n=3n=3n=3 the tutorial proves it irreducible.

In the example, the invariant subspace came with an invariant complement. That this always happens for finite groups is Maschke’s theorem, and it is a genuine theorem: for the infinite group (Z,+)(\Z,+)(Z,+) it fails, as the self-study example at the end of Section 4.3 shows. The proof goes through unitarity.

Unitarity: the averaging trick

Equip VVV with a Hermitian inner product ⟨⋅,⋅⟩\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}⟨⋅,⋅⟩, conjugate-linear in the first (left) argument (the physics convention). An operator UUU is unitary if ⟨Uu,Uv⟩=⟨u,v⟩\inner{Uu}{Uv}=\inner{u}{v}⟨Uu,Uv⟩=⟨u,v⟩ for all u,v∈Vu,v\in Vu,v∈V, and a representation is unitary (with respect to the given product) if every ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) is. In quantum mechanics this is the natural demand: a symmetry must not change transition probabilities.

Theorem 4.3 (Unitarisability). Let (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V) be a representation of a finite group GGG, and let ⟨⋅,⋅⟩0\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}_0⟨⋅,⋅⟩0​ be any inner product on VVV. Then ⟨u,v⟩  :=  1∣G∣∑h∈G⟨ρ(h)u, ρ(h)v⟩0\inner{u}{v}\;:=\;\frac{1}{\abs G}\sum_{h\in G} \inner{\rho(h)u}{\,\rho(h)v}_0⟨u,v⟩:=∣G∣1​h∈G∑​⟨ρ(h)u,ρ(h)v⟩0​ is an inner product on VVV with respect to which ρ\rhoρ is unitary.

Proof. Sesquilinearity (conjugate-linearity in the first argument and linearity in the second) is inherited term by term, and positivity holds because a sum of nonnegative terms vanishes only if each term does — in particular the h=eh=eh=e term ⟨u,u⟩0\inner{u}{u}_0⟨u,u⟩0​, so ⟨u,u⟩=0\inner{u}{u}=0⟨u,u⟩=0 forces u=0u=0u=0.

For unitarity, fix ggg and compute ⟨ρ(g)u,ρ(g)v⟩=1∣G∣∑h∈G⟨ρ(hg)u,ρ(hg)v⟩0=1∣G∣∑h′∈G⟨ρ(h′)u,ρ(h′)v⟩0=⟨u,v⟩,\inner{\rho(g)u}{\rho(g)v} =\frac{1}{\abs G}\sum_{h\in G}\inner{\rho(hg)u}{\rho(hg)v}_0 =\frac{1}{\abs G}\sum_{h'\in G}\inner{\rho(h')u}{\rho(h')v}_0 =\inner{u}{v},⟨ρ(g)u,ρ(g)v⟩=∣G∣1​h∈G∑​⟨ρ(hg)u,ρ(hg)v⟩0​=∣G∣1​h′∈G∑​⟨ρ(h′)u,ρ(h′)v⟩0​=⟨u,v⟩, because as hhh runs once over GGG, so does h′=hgh'=hgh′=hg: the map h↦hgh\mapsto hgh↦hg is a bijection of GGG (cancellation — the Latin-square property of the Cayley table, Lecture 1). 

Remark 4.4 (The averaging trick). Average over the group, and the average is invariant, because translating the summation variable only reshuffles the sum. This is the most reused idea of the subject: it gives the orthogonality of characters in Lecture 5, and in Part II, with the sum replaced by an integral, it carries the whole theory.

The theorem changes the inner product and leaves the operators alone. In a basis orthonormal for the new product, the matrices D(g)D(g)D(g) are unitary matrices in the ordinary sense. So every matrix representation of a finite group is equivalent to one by unitary matrices, and from here on we may and do assume the matrices unitary whenever that helps. The change of basis can be found explicitly, as follows.

How to find SSS. Apply Gram–Schmidt to the standard basis (e1,…,en)(e_1,\dots,e_n)(e1​,…,en​) in the invariant product and take the results as the columns of SSS. Because Gram–Schmidt builds the jjj-th vector out of the first jjj, this SSS is upper triangular, and M=(S−1)†S−1M=(S^{-1})^{\dagger}S^{-1}M=(S−1)†S−1 is the Cholesky factorisation of MMM. It is convenient to compute with a computer algebra system.

Corollary 4.7. In a unitary representation, the orthogonal complement of an invariant subspace is invariant.

Proof. Let WWW be invariant and w′∈W⊥w'\in W^{\perp}w′∈W⊥. Maschke’s theorem: complete reducibility

Theorem 4.8 (Maschke). Every finite-dimensional complex representation of a finite group is a direct sum of irreducible subrepresentations: there are invariant subspaces W1,…,WkW_1,\dots,W_kW1​,…,Wk​, each irreducible, with V=W1⊕W2⊕⋯⊕Wk.V=W_1\oplus W_2\oplus\dots\oplus W_k .V=W1​⊕W2​⊕⋯⊕Wk​.

Proof. Induct on dim⁡V\dim VdimV. If dim⁡V=0\dim V=0dimV=0 the statement is empty (the direct sum of no representations), so assume dim⁡V≥1\dim V\geq1dimV≥1. If VVV is irreducible (in particular if dim⁡V=1\dim V=1dimV=1), there is nothing to prove.

Otherwise, equip VVV with an invariant inner product (Theorem 4.3) and let WWW be an invariant subspace with 0≠W≠V0\neq W\neq V0=W=V. By Corollary 4.7, V=W⊕W⊥V=W\oplus W^{\perp}V=W⊕W⊥ with both summands invariant and of strictly smaller dimension; apply the induction hypothesis to each. 

In matrix language: there is a single invertible SSS such that S D(g) S−1=(D(i1)(g)D(i2)(g)⋱)for every g∈GS\,D(g)\,S^{-1}= \begin{pmatrix} D^{(i_1)}(g) & & \\ & D^{(i_2)}(g) & \\ & & \ddots \end{pmatrix} \qquad\text{\emph{for every} } g\in GSD(g)S−1=​D(i1​)(g)​D(i2​)(g)​⋱​​for every g∈G simultaneously, with each diagonal block an irreducible matrix representation (Figure 4.1). The force of the statement lies in the word simultaneously: there is one basis in which every group element takes the same block form at the same time. Finding that basis for representations of physical interest is, in a practical sense, what Lectures 5 to 7 are about.

What each hypothesis is for [self-study]

Both hypotheses of Maschke’s theorem are necessary. First, finiteness of the group.

Example 4.9 (Reducible but indecomposable). Let the infinite group (Z,+)(\Z,+)(Z,+) be represented on C2\C^2C2 by the operators whose matrices in the basis (e1,e2)(e_1,e_2)(e1​,e2​) are D(n)=(1n01),D(n)D(m)=(1n+m01)=D(n+m).D(n)= \begin{pmatrix} 1&n\\ 0&1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad D(n)D(m)= \begin{pmatrix} 1&n+m\\ 0&1 \end{pmatrix}=D(n+m).D(n)=(10​n1​),D(n)D(m)=(10​n+m1​)=D(n+m). The line Ce1\C e_1Ce1​ is invariant, so the representation is reducible. But no complementary invariant line exists: any line other than Ce1\C e_1Ce1​ is spanned by some (a1)\binom{a}{1}(1a​), and D(1)(a1)=(1101)(a1)=(a+11)D(1)\binom{a}{1}=\begin{pmatrix} 1&1\\ 0&1 \end{pmatrix}\binom{a}{1}=\binom{a+1}{1}D(1)(1a​)=(10​11​)(1a​)=(1a+1​) is never proportional to (a1)\binom{a}{1}(1a​). The representation cannot be written as a direct sum of smaller ones — the matrices can be made simultaneously triangular but never simultaneously block-diagonal. There is no finite sum to average over, and no invariant inner product exists.

Second, the field. Over fields of prime characteristic ppp dividing ∣G∣\abs G∣G∣, the averaging factor 1/∣G∣1/\abs G1/∣G∣ does not exist and complete reducibility fails; what survives in that case is the subject called modular representation theory. Over R\RR, by contrast, the proof goes through verbatim with real inner products — though, as the rotations of C3C_3C3​ showed in Lecture 3, the irreducible pieces over R\RR and over C\CC can differ. In Part II, compactness of the Lie group will play the role finiteness plays here.

Remark 4.10 (The geometry of the standard representation). The decomposition has a picture (Figure 4.2). S3S_3S3​ permutes the coordinate axes of R3\R^3R3; the diagonal direction u0u_0u0​ is fixed, and the action turns the perpendicular plane V0V_0V0​ into itself. One group, the same irreducible two-dimensional representation, two coordinate descriptions.

Schur’s lemma

Maschke’s theorem breaks every representation into irreducible pieces. How precise is this decomposition? The answer comes from the maps between them. Recall from Lecture 3 that an intertwiner between representations (ρV,V)(\rho_V,V)(ρV​,V) and (ρW,W)(\rho_W,W)(ρW​,W) of GGG is a linear map T ⁣:V→WT\colon V\to WT:V→W with T ρV(g)=ρW(g) Tfor all g∈G.T\,\rho_V(g)=\rho_W(g)\,T \quad\text{for all }g\in G .TρV​(g)=ρW​(g)Tfor all g∈G. There we required TTT invertible and called the representations equivalent. Now we drop invertibility and study the vector space of all intertwiners, denoted Hom⁡G(V,W)\Hom_G(V,W)HomG​(V,W). The most important example in physics has V=WV=WV=W: an operator that commutes with every symmetry operation, such as a symmetric Hamiltonian. The decisive observation is one line long:

Lemma 4.11. If T∈Hom⁡G(V,W)T\in\Hom_G(V,W)T∈HomG​(V,W), then ker⁡T\ker TkerT is an invariant subspace of VVV and im⁡T\im TimT is an invariant subspace of WWW.

Proof. If Tv=0Tv=0Tv=0 then TρV(g)v=ρW(g)Tv=0T\rho_V(g)v=\rho_W(g)Tv=0TρV​(g)v=ρW​(g)Tv=0, so ρV(g)v∈ker⁡T\rho_V(g)v\in\ker TρV​(g)v∈kerT. And ρW(g) Tv=TρV(g)v∈im⁡T\rho_W(g)\,Tv=T\rho_V(g)v\in\im TρW​(g)Tv=TρV​(g)v∈imT. 

An irreducible representation has no invariant subspaces other than the zero subspace and the whole space. For irreducible representations the lemma therefore leaves only two possibilities.

Theorem 4.12 (Schur’s lemma, first form). Let VVV and WWW be irreducible representations of GGG and T∈Hom⁡G(V,W)T\in\Hom_G(V,W)T∈HomG​(V,W). Then either T=0T=0T=0 or TTT is an isomorphism (so V≅WV\cong WV≅W). In particular, between inequivalent irreducible representations the only intertwiner is zero.

Proof. Suppose T≠0T\neq0T=0. By Lemma 4.11, ker⁡T\ker TkerT is an invariant subspace of the irreducible VVV, and ker⁡T≠V\ker T\neq VkerT=V since T≠0T\neq0T=0; hence ker⁡T={0}\ker T=\{0\}kerT={0} and TTT is injective.

Likewise im⁡T\im TimT is a nonzero invariant subspace of the irreducible WWW, hence im⁡T=W\im T=WimT=W. So TTT is surjective. 

Theorem 4.13 (Schur’s lemma, second form). Let (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V) be a finite-dimensional complex irreducible representation, and let T∈Hom⁡G(V,V)T\in\Hom_G(V,V)T∈HomG​(V,V), i.e. Tρ(g)=ρ(g)TT\rho(g)=\rho(g)TTρ(g)=ρ(g)T for all g∈Gg\in Gg∈G. Then T=λid⁡VT=\lambda\id_VT=λidV​ for some λ∈C\lambda\in\Cλ∈C: Hom⁡G(V,V)=C id⁡V.\Hom_G(V,V)=\C\,\id_V .HomG​(V,V)=CidV​.

Proof. Because C\CC is algebraically closed, the characteristic polynomial of TTT has a root. So TTT has an eigenvalue λ∈C\lambda\in\Cλ∈C.

Then T−λid⁡VT-\lambda\id_VT−λidV​ is again an intertwiner, since sums and scalar multiples of intertwiners intertwine, and id⁡V\id_VidV​ commutes with every ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g). The kernel of T−λid⁡VT-\lambda\id_VT−λidV​ is the eigenspace of λ\lambdaλ, so this kernel is nonzero.

By Theorem 4.12 a non-invertible intertwiner from VVV to VVV must vanish. Applied to T−λid⁡VT-\lambda\id_VT−λidV​, this gives T=λid⁡VT=\lambda\id_VT=λidV​. 

In words: the eigenspace of TTT is invariant and not {0}\{0\}{0}, and an irreducible representation has no invariant subspaces but {0}\{0\}{0} and the whole space. So the eigenspace is everything.

Proof. If V≇WV\not\cong WV≅W this is the first form. If V≅WV\cong WV≅W, fix an isomorphism T0∈Hom⁡G(V,W)T_0\in\Hom_G(V,W)T0​∈HomG​(V,W); for any T∈Hom⁡G(V,W)T\in\Hom_G(V,W)T∈HomG​(V,W) the composite T0−1T∈Hom⁡G(V,V)T_0^{-1}T\in\Hom_G(V,V)T0−1​T∈HomG​(V,V) is a scalar λid⁡\lambda\idλid by the second form, so T=λ T0T=\lambda\,T_0T=λT0​. 

Corollary 4.15 (Abelian groups). Every complex irreducible representation of an abelian group is one-dimensional. In particular the nnn representations χk(rm)=e2πikm/n\chi_k(r^m)=e^{2\pi\ii km/n}χk​(rm)=e2πikm/n of Lecture 3 are all the irreducible representations of CnC_nCn​.

Proof. Let (ρ,V)(\rho,V)(ρ,V) be irreducible and GGG abelian. For fixed h∈Gh\in Gh∈G the operator ρ(h)\rho(h)ρ(h) commutes with every ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) — that is, ρ(h)∈Hom⁡G(V,V)\rho(h)\in\Hom_G(V,V)ρ(h)∈HomG​(V,V) — so ρ(h)=λhid⁡V\rho(h)=\lambda_h\id_Vρ(h)=λh​idV​ by the second form. For G=CnG=C_nG=Cn​, a one-dimensional representation is fixed by the number ρ(r)\rho(r)ρ(r), and ρ(r)n=ρ(rn)=1\rho(r)^n=\rho(r^n)=1ρ(r)n=ρ(rn)=1 makes it an nnn-th root of unity, ρ(r)=e2πik/n\rho(r)=e^{2\pi\ii k/n}ρ(r)=e2πik/n for some kkk. That is χk\chi_kχk​. 

So the rotation representation of C3C_3C3​ had to split over C\CC, as Lecture 3 found by hand. Over R\RR the proof fails, for want of an eigenvalue; “irreducible” in this course means irreducible over C\CC.

Remark 4.16 (Schur’s lemma over the real numbers). The second form fails over R\RR, where eigenvalues need not exist. Let C4=⟨r⟩C_4=\langle r\rangleC4​=⟨r⟩ act irreducibly on R2\R^2R2 by the quarter-turn J=R(π/2)=(0−110)J=R(\pi/2)=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)J=R(π/2)=(01​−10​). The commutant is a copy of the field C\CC among the real 2×22\times22×2 matrices, with JJJ playing the role of i\iii. Over C\CC the representation splits into χ1⊕χ−1\chi_1\oplus\chi_{-1}χ1​⊕χ−1​, and Schur’s lemma holds again.

Read physically, the second form says that an operator commuting with an irreducible symmetry action is a multiple of the identity. A Hamiltonian commuting with the symmetry is therefore a single number on an irreducible representation that occurs once: one energy for the whole representation, so the level’s degeneracy is at least dim⁡V(i)\dim V^{(i)}dimV(i). That degeneracy is enforced by the symmetry. The level can be more degenerate still, if another representation happens to have the same energy. Lecture 6 makes this precise, including accidental degeneracy and the case of repeated irreducibles.

For example, let an atom sit at the centre of an equilateral triangle (Figure 4.3). Its orbitals 2px2p_x2px​ and 2py2p_y2py​ transform like the coordinates xxx and yyy (Lecture 3), so the rotation rrr carries 2px2p_x2px​ to the superposition ρ(r) 2px=−12 2px+32 2py.\rho(r)\,2p_x=-\frac12\,2p_x+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\,2p_y .ρ(r)2px​=−21​2px​+23​​2py​. Let 2px2p_x2px​ and 2py2p_y2py​ be stationary states with energies ExE_xEx​ and EyE_yEy​. The Hamiltonian commutes with ρ(r)\rho(r)ρ(r), so ρ(r) 2px\rho(r)\,2p_xρ(r)2px​ is a stationary state with energy ExE_xEx​. This superposition is a stationary state only if Ex=EyE_x=E_yEx​=Ey​. So the symmetry forces the two orbitals to have the same energy.

We can now answer the question this section opened with: how precise is Maschke’s decomposition? The subspaces WjW_jWj​ are not unique, but the multiplicities are. Let V=W1⊕⋯⊕WkV=W_1\oplus\dots\oplus W_kV=W1​⊕⋯⊕Wk​ with every WjW_jWj​ irreducible. The projections onto the summands WjW_jWj​ are intertwiners, because each WjW_jWj​ is invariant. So an intertwiner from V(i)V^{(i)}V(i) to VVV splits into one piece for each WjW_jWj​, and by Corollary 4.14 dim⁡Hom⁡G(V(i),V)=∑j=1kdim⁡Hom⁡G(V(i),Wj)=mi.\dim\Hom_G\bigl(V^{(i)},V\bigr) =\sum_{j=1}^{k}\dim\Hom_G\bigl(V^{(i)},W_j\bigr) =m_i .dimHomG​(V(i),V)=j=1∑k​dimHomG​(V(i),Wj​)=mi​. The left-hand side is defined without choosing any decomposition, so every decomposition gives the same multiplicities. Lecture 5 computes them without hunting for invariant subspaces.

Summary

  • Irreducibility. An invariant subspace is one preserved by GGG; a representation is irreducible when it has no proper invariant subspace (Definition 4.1).
  • Averaging. Every representation of a finite group is unitary for the averaged inner product (Theorem 4.3); the average is invariant because h↦hgh\mapsto hgh↦hg only reorders the sum.
  • Maschke. In a unitary representation the orthogonal complement of an invariant subspace is invariant, so every representation of a finite group is a direct sum of irreducibles, V≅⨁imiV(i)V\cong\bigoplus_i m_iV^{(i)}V≅⨁i​mi​V(i) (Theorem 4.8).
  • Schur’s lemma. An intertwiner of irreducibles is zero or an isomorphism (Theorem 4.12); over C\CC, an operator commuting with an irreducible representation is a scalar (Theorem 4.13). So abelian groups have only one-dimensional irreducibles, and the χk\chi_kχk​ are all those of CnC_nCn​ (Corollary 4.15). The multiplicities mi=dim⁡Hom⁡G(V(i),V)m_i=\dim\Hom_G(V^{(i)},V)mi​=dimHomG​(V(i),V) do not depend on the decomposition.
