Lecture 6

Symmetry, projectors, and degeneracy

Symmetry tells us the degeneracies of a Hamiltonian before we diagonalise it. This lecture shows how. We first say precisely when a group is a symmetry of a quantum system: its operators commute with the Hamiltonian. Characters then give projectors that split any representation into its isotypic components. An operator that commutes with the group is block-diagonal in a basis adapted to the symmetry, and each of its eigenvalues comes in a multiplet whose size is the dimension of an irreducible representation. We prove this once. Here we apply it to a quantum particle hopping on a triangle, and in Lecture 7 to the vibrations of a molecule.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Say when a group is a symmetry of a quantum system.
  • Project a representation onto its isotypic components.
  • Predict degeneracies from the block-diagonalisation theorem.

Symmetry in quantum mechanics

The state space of a quantum system is a complex vector space, and a symmetry acts on it linearly, by the function-space construction of Lecture 3: (ρ(g)ψ)(x⃗ )=ψ(g−1x⃗ ).\bigl(\rho(g)\psi\bigr)(\vec x\,)=\psi\bigl(g^{-1}\vec x\,\bigr).(ρ(g)ψ)(x)=ψ(g−1x). For ggg orthogonal this preserves the norm of square-integrable functions, so ρ\rhoρ is a unitary representation. (Wigner’s theorem, met in Part II, says that every symmetry of quantum mechanics is implemented by a unitary or an antiunitary operator; for point groups the unitary action above is all we need.)

Definition 6.1 (Quantum symmetry). Let ρ\rhoρ be a unitary representation of a group on the state space. The group GGG is a symmetry of the quantum system with Hamiltonian HHH if [ρ(g), H]=0for all g∈G.\bigl[\rho(g),\,H\bigr]=0 \qquad\text{for all }g\in G .[ρ(g),H]=0for all g∈G. In the language of Lecture 3, HHH is an intertwiner of the state space with itself.

For an unbounded HHH the commutator is meant on a common domain of smooth, compactly supported functions, that is, functions that vanish outside some ball of finite radius. Every ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g) preserves this domain, because a rotation or a reflection maps a ball to a ball of the same radius. That the commutator then vanishes on the whole domain of HHH, eigenfunctions included, we take for granted.

Proposition 6.2. Let H=−ℏ22m∇2+V(x⃗ )H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+V(\vec x\,)H=−2mℏ2​∇2+V(x), with the group acting by the function-space action above. Then G⊂O(3)G\subset \Orth(3)G⊂O(3) is a symmetry if and only if the potential is invariant: V(gx⃗ )=V(x⃗ )V(g\vec x\,)=V(\vec x\,)V(gx)=V(x) for all g∈Gg\in Gg∈G.

The kinetic term commutes with every rotation and reflection, because the Laplacian does; so only the potential can break the symmetry. The self-study section at the end of the lecture checks the Laplacian.

Proposition 6.3. If [ρ(g),H]=0[\rho(g),H]=0[ρ(g),H]=0 for all ggg, then every energy eigenspace {ψ:Hψ=Eψ}\{\psi:H\psi=E\psi\}{ψ:Hψ=Eψ} is an invariant subspace. So each eigenspace carries a representation of GGG.

Proof. Hρ(g)ψ=ρ(g)Hψ=E ρ(g)ψH\rho(g)\psi=\rho(g)H\psi=E\,\rho(g)\psiHρ(g)ψ=ρ(g)Hψ=Eρ(g)ψ. 

So the energy levels of a symmetric system are representations of its symmetry group. Which representations, and how large? Answering that is the business of this lecture.

In classical mechanics a symmetry maps solutions of the equations of motion to solutions, and it does no more. A finite group in particular conserves nothing: conservation laws come from continuous families of symmetries, by Noether’s theorem, which Part II proves. Quantum mechanics gets more out of a discrete symmetry. The reason is the superposition principle: the symmetry acts on a vector space, and this lets us apply the theory of linear representations.

Characters compute not only multiplicities: together with the operators ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g), they also find subspaces. The block-diagonalisation theorem

Now let an operator AAA on VVV commute with the group, Aρ(g)=ρ(g)AA\rho(g)=\rho(g)AAρ(g)=ρ(g)A for all ggg. Physically, AAA is a Hamiltonian, or the dynamical matrix of Lecture 7, that the symmetry leaves unchanged. Such an AAA commutes with every PiP_iPi​, since PiP_iPi​ is a combination of the ρ(g)\rho(g)ρ(g). So AAA maps each isotypic component into itself, and it has no matrix elements between different components. What does it do inside one?

Choose a symmetry-adapted basis of ViV_iVi​: split ViV_iVi​ into its mim_imi​ copies of V(i)V^{(i)}V(i), and in the aaath copy choose vectors w1(a),…,wni(a)w^{(a)}_1,\dots,w^{(a)}_{n_i}w1(a)​,…,wni​(a)​ that transform by the same matrices in every copy, ρ(g) wν(a)=∑μDμν(i)(g) wμ(a),a=1,…,mi.\rho(g)\,w^{(a)}_\nu=\sum_{\mu}D^{(i)}_{\mu\nu}(g)\,w^{(a)}_\mu, \qquad a=1,\dots,m_i.ρ(g)wν(a)​=μ∑​Dμν(i)​(g)wμ(a)​,a=1,…,mi​. Such a basis exists: choose a basis of the first copy, and carry it to the other copies by isomorphisms of representations. With an invariant inner product (Lecture 4) it can be chosen orthonormal: take the copies mutually orthogonal and the isomorphisms unitary. Schur’s lemma allows the latter, because an isomorphism TTT between unitary irreducibles has T†T=c id⁡T^\dagger T=c\,\idT†T=cid with c>0c>0c>0, so T/cT/\sqrt cT/c​ is unitary. The index μ\muμ is called the partner index, and aaa counts the copies.

In practice the copies are usually built alike. Suppose two sets of basis vectors are permuted by the group in the same way. The map that sends each vector of the first set to its counterpart in the second is then an isomorphism of representations, so the same linear combination, taken in each set, gives vectors that transform by the same matrices. Section 6.4 and Lecture 7 build their adapted bases this way.

Note how the work is divided. Group theory fixes the block structure — the sizes mim_imi​, the multiplet sizes nin_ini​, the adapted basis — and the dynamics only fills in the small matrices F(i)F^{(i)}F(i). An eigenvalue problem of size dim⁡V\dim VdimV splits into independent problems of sizes mim_imi​. An irreducible that occurs once needs no diagonalisation at all: its F(i)F^{(i)}F(i) is a number, and its adapted vectors are eigenvectors. Section 6.4 shows both cases: in its first example every F(i)F^{(i)}F(i) is a number, in its second a genuine 2×22\times22×2 matrix.

For a Hamiltonian, the lower bound in the next corollary needs only Proposition 6.3, even on an infinite-dimensional state space. A finite-dimensional energy eigenspace and an isotypic component are both invariant, so their intersection is too. If it is not zero, it contains an irreducible invariant subspace of type iii, of dimension nin_ini​. The second statement is the theorem itself, for a Hamiltonian on a finite-dimensional space, such as the orbitals of the next section.

Corollary 6.8 (Symmetry-enforced degeneracy). Every energy level in the isotypic component of type iii is at least nin_ini​-fold degenerate, ni=dim⁡V(i)n_i=\dim V^{(i)}ni​=dimV(i). If the eigenvalues of each reduced matrix are simple and no two blocks share an eigenvalue, each energy level is exactly one irreducible multiplet.

Lecture 4 called a degeneracy that the symmetry dictates enforced, and promised to tell it apart from an accidental one. We can now do so precisely. A degeneracy of nin_ini​ is enforced. A larger one is accidental: an eigenvalue repeated inside one F(i)F^{(i)}F(i), or two blocks that happen to share one. A generic small perturbation that keeps the symmetry removes an accidental degeneracy — unless a symmetry not yet taken into account enforces it. The dimensions of the irreducible representations are the sizes of the multiplets in a symmetric spectrum.

Real, complex and quaternionic irreducibles. In the language of real types (Frobenius–Schur theory, stated without proof): an irreducible is of real type if it is equivalent to one by real matrices, of complex type if it is not equivalent to its conjugate, and of quaternionic type if it is equivalent to its conjugate but has no real form. The three cases are told apart by 1∣G∣∑gχ(g2)=1,0,−1\frac1{\abs G}\sum_g\chi(g^2)=1,0,-1∣G∣1​∑g​χ(g2)=1,0,−1. Hydrogen. The levels of the hydrogen atom are n2n^2n2-fold degenerate (ignoring spin), far more than the (2ℓ+1)(2\ell+1)(2ℓ+1)-fold multiplets of the rotation group. Summary

  • Quantum symmetry. GGG is a symmetry when [ρ(g),H]=0[\rho(g),H]=0[ρ(g),H]=0; then every energy eigenspace carries a representation of GGG (Definition 6.1, Proposition 6.3).
  • Projectors. Pi=ni∣G∣∑gχi(g)‾ρ(g)P_i=\frac{n_i}{\abs G}\sum_g\overline{\chi_i(g)}\rho(g)Pi​=∣G∣ni​​∑g​χi​(g)​ρ(g) projects onto the isotypic component ViV_iVi​, and V=⨁iViV=\bigoplus_iV_iV=⨁i​Vi​ (Theorem 6.5).
  • Block diagonalisation. An operator commuting with GGG acts on ViV_iVi​ through an mi×mim_i\times m_imi​×mi​ matrix F(i)F^{(i)}F(i), the same for every partner; its eigenvalues are at least nin_ini​-fold degenerate (Theorem 6.7). Group theory fixes the blocks, dynamics fills them in.
  • Degeneracy. A level of type iii is at least nin_ini​-fold degenerate, and generically exactly (Corollary 6.8); more is accidental, or a sign of reality or of a larger symmetry.
  • The triangle. H=−2tPA1+tPEH=-2tP_{A_1}+tP_EH=−2tPA1​​+tPE​: one level at −2t-2t−2t and an enforced doublet at ttt. On two stacked triangles each irreducible occurs twice, and two 2×22\times22×2 reduced matrices give the singlets −2t∓t′-2t\mp t'−2t∓t′ and the doublets t∓t′t\mp t't∓t′.
