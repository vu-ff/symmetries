Recommended

Four books, two for each part of the course: one written for physicists and one for mathematicians. None is required, but if you buy one book, buy Jones.

  • H. F. Jones, Groups, Representations and Physics, 2nd ed. Institute of Physics Publishing, 1998.

    The one book to have. Short, written for physicists, and it follows the course from finite groups to the Poincaré group in nearly the same order.

    Cited in Lectures 1–5, 11, 13, 15–16

  • J.-P. Serre, Linear Representations of Finite Groups. Springer (GTM 42), 1977.

    Part I only: about forty pages with the cleanest proofs of Schur's lemma, the orthogonality relations and character theory. The mathematician's companion to Lectures 3–5.

    Cited in Lectures 3–5

  • B. C. Hall, Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and Representations: An Elementary Introduction, 2nd ed. Springer (GTM 222), 2015.

    The mathematics of Part II done with matrices rather than manifolds: the exponential map, Lie algebras, sl(2, ℂ) and the SU(2)–SO(3) cover. The notes it grew from are free on arXiv (math-ph/0005032).

    Cited in Lectures 8–15

  • P. Woit, Quantum Theory, Groups and Representations: An Introduction. Springer, 2017.

    Quantum mechanics told as representation theory: spin, the Heisenberg group, the harmonic oscillator and the Lorentz group. Read it to see why Part II is physics.

    Cited in Lectures 9–12, 14–15

Secondary resources

Open these for one topic rather than reading them through. They are grouped by how they are written and how far they go. “Cited in” marks a book the notes refer to; “goes with”, one recommended alongside them.

Mathematics: first steps

If the mathematics in the notes feels fast. Start with Armstrong for groups, James–Liebeck for characters and Stillwell for Lie groups.

  • M. A. Armstrong, Groups and Symmetry. Springer (UTM), 1988.

    Groups through geometric examples; the place to go when cosets and quotients feel unfamiliar.

    Cited in Lectures 1–2

  • B. Steinberg, Representation Theory of Finite Groups: An Introductory Approach. Springer (Universitext), 2012.

    Representations of finite groups with nothing beyond linear algebra.

    Goes with Lectures 3–5

  • G. James and M. Liebeck, Representations and Characters of Groups. 2nd ed. Cambridge University Press, 2001.

    Character theory with many worked tables and solutions to every exercise.

    Goes with Lectures 3–6

  • J. Stillwell, Naive Lie Theory. Springer (UTM), 2008.

    Lie groups from the quaternions, with the least machinery possible.

    Cited in Lectures 8–9, 12

  • K. Erdmann and M. J. Wildon, Introduction to Lie Algebras. Springer (SUMS), 2006.

    Lie algebras as algebraic objects in their own right.

    Cited in Lecture 8

Mathematics: going deeper

Graduate mathematics, for when a proof in the notes leaves you wanting the whole theory. Try Fulton–Harris first; Etingof et al. is free.

  • W. Fulton and J. Harris, Representation Theory: A First Course. Springer (GTM 129), 1991.

    Representation theory learned through examples, from finite groups to the classical Lie algebras.

    Cited in Lectures 3–4, 11

  • P. Etingof, O. Golberg, S. Hensel, T. Liu, A. Schwendner, D. Vaintrob and E. Yudovina, Introduction to Representation Theory. American Mathematical Society (Student Mathematical Library 59), 2011.

    Short and problem-driven: algebras, sl(2), Schur–Weyl duality. Free on arXiv (0901.0827).

    Goes with Lectures 3–5, 11 free online

  • B. Simon, Representations of Finite and Compact Groups. American Mathematical Society (Graduate Studies in Mathematics 10), 1996.

    Finite and compact groups with full analytic rigour, through the Peter–Weyl theorem.

    Goes with Lectures 4–5, 13

  • R. Carter, G. Segal and I. Macdonald, Lectures on Lie Groups and Lie Algebras. Cambridge University Press (LMS Student Texts 32), 1995.

    Three short courses; the middle one, on Lie groups, continues Part II a level up.

    Goes with Lectures 9, 12–14

  • B. C. Hall, Quantum Theory for Mathematicians. Springer (GTM 267), 2013.

    Proves Stone's theorem and the Stone–von Neumann theorem, which the lectures only state.

    Goes with Lectures 10, 12, 14

Physics: first steps

Physicists' introductions, lighter on proof. Zee for a conversational tour of the whole course; Schwichtenberg for the big picture.

  • A. Zee, Group Theory in a Nutshell for Physicists. Princeton University Press, 2016.

    Conversational and physical, from characters to SU(3); strongest on spinors and the Lorentz group.

    Goes with Lectures 1–5, 11–12, 15

  • D. M. Bishop, Group Theory and Chemistry. Dover, 1993 (orig. Oxford University Press, 1973).

    Written for chemists: the gentlest route to point groups and molecular vibrations.

    Cited in Lecture 6

  • J. J. Sakurai and J. Napolitano, Modern Quantum Mechanics. 3rd ed. Cambridge University Press, 2021.

    Chapters 3–4 only: rotations, Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, the Wigner–Eckart theorem and discrete symmetries, in a book you may already own.

    Goes with Lectures 7, 12–13

  • N. Jeevanjee, An Introduction to Tensors and Group Theory for Physicists, 2nd ed. Birkhäuser, 2015.

    Tensors and Lie groups in the notation physicists use.

    Cited in Lecture 8

  • J. Schwichtenberg, Physics from Symmetry, 2nd ed. Springer, 2018.

    Motivation first: how symmetry shapes physical theories. For the big picture, not for proofs.

    Cited in Lecture 15

Physics: going deeper

Fuller physics treatments, and where to go after the course. Tung is the classic; Arovas's notes are free and cover both parts.

  • W.-K. Tung, Group Theory in Physics. World Scientific, 1985.

    The classic graduate text, systematic and strongest on rotations and the Lorentz and Poincaré groups. Its exercises are solved in M. Aivazis, Problems and Solutions (World Scientific, 1991).

    Cited in Lectures 1–5, 7, 11–13, 15–16

  • D. Arovas, Lecture Notes on Group Theory in Physics (A Work in Progress). University of California, San Diego, course notes.

    Free notes from the great orthogonality theorem to Wigner–Eckart; still being revised, so section numbers move.

    Goes with Lectures 3–7, 12–13 free online

  • S. Sternberg, Group Theory and Physics. Cambridge University Press, 1994.

    A geometer's view of vibrations, Haar measure and Wigner's classification.

    Goes with Lectures 6, 13, 16

  • R. Gilmore, Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and Some of Their Applications. Wiley, 1974 (repr. Dover, 2005).

    A large reference with many worked applications: dip in, don't read through.

    Cited in Lectures 8, 14

  • H. Osborn, Group Theory. University of Cambridge, Mathematical Tripos Part III lecture notes (available from the DAMTP lecture-notes pages); section numbers follow the version of 13 September 2021.

    Dense Cambridge Part III notes aimed at theoretical physicists.

    Cited in Lectures 10, 14 free online

  • M. Chaichian and R. Hagedorn, Symmetries in Quantum Mechanics: From Angular Momentum to Supersymmetry. Institute of Physics Publishing, 1998.

    Wigner's theorem, Noether's theorem, superselection rules and tensor operators.

    Goes with Lectures 10, 12–13

  • K. Sundermeyer, Symmetries in Fundamental Physics. Springer (Fundamental Theories of Physics 176), 2014.

    Symmetry followed through the whole of physics, from mechanics to gravity.

    Goes with Lectures 10, 15–16

  • M. Guidry and Y. Sun, Symmetry, Broken Symmetry, and Topology in Modern Physics: A First Course. Cambridge University Press, 2022.

    What comes after the course: symmetry breaking, gauge theories and topological matter.

    Goes with Lectures 11–12, 15–16

  • P. Ramond, Group Theory: A Physicist’s Survey. Cambridge University Press, 2010.

    A compact survey reaching far beyond the course; Lecture 16 uses its treatment of the Poincaré Casimirs.

    Cited in Lecture 16

Applications: molecules, crystals, particles

Group theory at work on molecules, crystals, atoms and particles. Tinkham for the classic account; Dresselhaus et al. for a modern, fully worked one.

  • M. S. Dresselhaus, G. Dresselhaus and A. Jorio, Group Theory: Application to the Physics of Condensed Matter. Springer, 2008.

    Crystal fields, selection rules, and infrared and Raman activity, with problems throughout.

    Goes with Lectures 5–7

  • T. Inui, Y. Tanabe and Y. Onodera, Group Theory and Its Applications in Physics. Springer (Series in Solid-State Sciences 78), 1990.

    Molecules and crystals with proofs: space groups, electronic states and time reversal.

    Goes with Lectures 5–7

  • M. Tinkham, Group Theory and Quantum Mechanics. McGraw–Hill, 1964 (repr. Dover, 2003).

    The classic: vibrations, selection rules and the Wigner–Eckart theorem.

    Cited in Lectures 6–7

  • J. P. Elliott and P. G. Dawber, Symmetry in Physics, 2 vols. Macmillan, 1979.

    Two volumes of physics applications: vibrations, rotations and angular-momentum coupling.

    Cited in Lectures 6–7, 12–13

  • G. Costa and G. Fogli, Symmetries and Group Theory in Particle Physics. Springer (Lecture Notes in Physics 823), 2012.

    Rotations, the Lorentz and Poincaré groups, and the unitary groups of the quark model.

    Cited in Lectures 12, 15

  • D. A. Varshalovich, A. N. Moskalev and V. K. Khersonskii, Quantum Theory of Angular Momentum. World Scientific, 1988.

    A handbook of D-functions and Clebsch–Gordan coefficients in one phase convention: look formulas up, don't read it.

    Goes with Lectures 12–13

  • Z.-Q. Ma, Group Theory for Physicists. World Scientific, 2007.

    Clebsch–Gordan coefficients and other computations worked out in detail.

    Cited in Lecture 13

Sources behind single results

Research articles and monographs that a lecture cites for one precise result — a theorem stated without proof, or the experiment behind a claim. Not reading for the course; the place to check it.

  • V. Bargmann, On unitary ray representations of continuous groups. Annals of Mathematics 59 (1954), 1–46.

    Cited in Lecture 12

  • S. Weinberg, The Quantum Theory of Fields, Vol. I: Foundations. Cambridge University Press, 1995. Section 5.7 treats causal fields and the relativistic spin–statistics connection, a theorem not derived from a rotation phase in this course.

    Cited in Lecture 12

  • H. Rauch, A. Zeilinger, G. Badurek, A. Wilfing, W. Bauspiess, and U. Bonse, Verification of Coherent Spinor Rotation of Fermions. Physics Letters A 54 (1975), 425–427. DOI: 10.1016/0375-9601(75)90798-7.

    Cited in Lecture 12

  • S. A. Werner, R. Colella, A. W. Overhauser, and C. F. Eagen, Observation of the Phase Shift of a Neutron Due to Precession in a Magnetic Field. Physical Review Letters 35 (1975), 1053–1055. DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevLett.35.1053.

    Cited in Lecture 12

  • E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Fourier Analysis: An Introduction. Princeton University Press (Princeton Lectures in Analysis I), 2003. Chapter 3, Section 1, Theorem 1.1, pp. 69–79 gives the mean-square completeness result used in Lecture 13.

    Cited in Lecture 13

  • M. Reed and B. Simon, Methods of Modern Mathematical Physics, Vol. I: Functional Analysis, rev. ed. Academic Press, 1980. Chapter VIII, Theorems VIII.5, VIII.8, VIII.10, and VIII.13 and Section VIII.5, Example 2 cover spectral calculus, Stone’s theorem, invariant-domain generators, strong commutation, and the bounded-CCR obstruction.

    Cited in Lecture 14

  • G. B. Folland, Harmonic Analysis in Phase Space. Princeton University Press (Annals of Mathematics Studies 122), 1989. Chapter 1, Sections 2–5, including Theorem 1.50, treats the Heisenberg group, Schrödinger/Weyl representation, and Stone–von Neumann theorem.

    Cited in Lecture 14

  • O. Bratteli and D. W. Robinson, Operator Algebras and Quantum Statistical Mechanics 2: Equilibrium States, Models in Quantum Statistical Mechanics, 2nd ed. (1997), 2nd printing. Springer, 2002. Section 5.2.1, pp. 7–8 gives Fock-space second quantisation, its finite-particle analytic core and closure, and the lifted unitary group.

    Cited in Lecture 14

  • A. M. L. Messiah and O. W. Greenberg, Symmetrization Postulate and Its Experimental Foundation. Physical Review 136 (1964), B248–B267. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.136.B248.

    Cited in Lecture 14

  • A. O. Barut and R. Rączka, Theory of Group Representations and Applications, 2nd ed. World Scientific, 1986.

    Cited in Lecture 16

  • R. Berndt, Representations of Linear Groups: An Introduction Based on Examples from Physics and Number Theory. Vieweg, 2007. DOI: 10.1007/978-3-8348-9401-4. Theorem 5.2, p. 62 gives the SNAG theorem used for the translation subgroup.

    Cited in Lecture 16

  • G. B. Folland, A Course in Abstract Harmonic Analysis, 2nd ed. Chapman & Hall/CRC, 2016. Section 6.6, Theorem 6.42, pp. 199–201 gives the regular semidirect-product Mackey theorem, including induced irreducibility, exhaustivity, and equivalence.

    Cited in Lecture 16

  • J. B. Conway, A Course in Functional Analysis, 1st ed. Springer (GTM 96), 1985. Chapter IX, Theorems 6.4 and 6.6 give the strong/weak bicommutant closure and the multiplication maximal-abelian theorem for sigma-finite measure spaces.

What to read for each lecture

Each lecture's own reading list, as it closes the lecture notes. Chapter and section numbers are for the editions listed above. A lecture's list appears here when the lecture opens.

Lecture Reading
1 Groups, examples, and the landscape of finite groups

Armstrong, Groups and Symmetry (groups, subgroups, Lagrange’s theorem); Jones, Groups, Representations and Physics, Ch. 1, §2.2; Tung, Group Theory in Physics, §§2.1–2.3, 2.5.
2 Structure of groups: homomorphisms, quotients, and products

Armstrong, Groups and Symmetry (cosets, quotient groups, direct and semidirect products); Jones, Groups, Representations and Physics, Ch. 2; Tung, Group Theory in Physics, §§2.4–2.7.
3 Linear representations and complete reducibility

Jones, Groups, Representations and Physics, Ch. 3; Tung, Group Theory in Physics, §§3.1–3.3; Fulton–Harris, Representation Theory, §§1.1–1.2; Serre, Linear Representations of Finite Groups, §§1.1–1.4.
4 Schur’s lemma, orthogonality, and the group algebra

Serre, Linear Representations of Finite Groups, §§2.2–2.5 (Schur’s lemma and the orthogonality relations); Jones §§4.1–4.3; Tung §§3.4–3.5, 3.7; Fulton–Harris §§1.2, 2.2, 3.4.
